# LESSONS.md

Every mistake this project has already paid for once, and what it cost.

**Split out of `CLAUDE.md` on 2026-08-06.** That file is attached to every session and this
one is not, which is the entire point: the lessons are worth keeping and are not worth paying
for on a turn that never goes near their subject. `CLAUDE.md` still owns the rules, the
settled decisions and the reasoning; this owns the scar tissue.

**Read the part that matches what you are about to change.** The single most common shape of
bug in this codebase is a value derived somewhere other than the one table that owns it.

## Creature models and box nets

- **`Get-LocalGroupMember` can silently return nothing for a non-admin caller**, which makes an "is this user an admin?" check produce a false negative for the wrong reason. `net localgroup Administrators` works from a standard account and gives the real answer.
- **The reference-art boundary is mechanical, not remembered, and the incident behind it is worth knowing.** The rule: **reference art may be measured from, must never live under `assets/`, and must never ship in a release** — and every staging tool *refuses* to write under `assets/` so it cannot be forgotten. It exists because a Minecraft asset mirror was once dropped into `assets/textures/blocks/`, and because the build copies `assets/` wholesale, that silently put 925 of Mojang's PNGs into the output folder. Reference material lives in `reference/`, gitignored and outside `assets/`.
- **A face mapping that is wrong stays invisible until an asset disagrees with itself.** Creature boxes read rectangles out of an unwrapped skin net, and the quarter-turn mapping for a lying body had world top and bottom swapped. On a sheep that is undetectable — its belly and back are both plain fleece — and it surfaced only on a cow, which has a pink underside, painted across its spine. The same shape of bug put a face on all six sides of every head for a whole milestone. **When a mapping is wrong, look for the first asset where the two sides disagree; until then it looks perfect.**
- **Passing every metric is not the same as looking right, and this was measured rather than supposed.** A deliberately flattened pig scored **correlation 0.76 and mask agreement 79.1** against the reference - *beating* the properly modelled version's 0.66 and 72.7. Four faults that were obvious on sight - a slab body, a zip up the spine, a halo on the face, panel borders on every rect - passed every check that existed at the time, and the user found each in seconds. **The table catches gross deviation; the eye is the gate.** `tools/compare-skin.ps1` now prints that warning above every run.
- **Shading must follow the form, never the rectangle.** Darkening the rim of each net rect looked like a cheap way to suggest roundness. On the model those rims meet, so every face gains a dark frame and the animal reads as a grid of tiles - the user called it a zipper. The reference vignettes across the *whole body*, indifferent to where one net rect ends and the next begins.
- **Put invented detail on a face the player cannot see, or do not invent it.** A seam painted down the pig's back rect was in view from every angle and read as a zip. The reference carries the same seam on its *belly* - the net's front rect, which the quarter turn puts underneath. Before adding detail, check which world face the rect actually becomes.
- **Identify an unknown texture region by measuring its colour, not by reasoning about what it ought to be.** Three unexplained nets in the cow sheet were settled in one command by averaging their pixels: pink was the udder, grey the horns, and a brown one was neither. The guess had been that brown net, which put a slab of hide through the animal's back.
- **A refactor from index to offset is exactly where silent breakage hides.** Skin slots were addressed as "skin 1" and became a texel row offset, because a cow's net is 64 rows where a sheep's is 32. Both are floats, so every remaining `1` compiled cleanly and meant *one texel down* rather than *thirty-two*. Nothing warned. **After changing what a parameter means, grep for the old literals rather than trusting the build.**
- **The biggest box is not necessarily the head.** The goat's 11×14×11 net sits where a head belongs and is the largest piece on the sheet; it is the woolly neck. What settled it was two measurements, not reasoning: its front rect is featureless white, and the eyes are on the *side* rects of a different, much narrower net — at `(37,58)` and `(55,58)`, which mirror exactly about that box's 10-texel depth. **Find the eyes before deciding what anything else is.**
- **A correct net does not make a correct animal; posture does.** Every one of the rabbit's box nets was measured right and it still rendered as a squat brick with its head buried in its chest, because every box was level. The user sent the reference render back with "shaped wrong" and they were right. Nothing about the dimensions changed — three tilts fixed it: the body pitched so the haunches ride high and the head sits low and forward, the head tipped slightly up, the ears raked back. The same failure named the horse bug: with a level head, the pale muzzle sits *beside* the eye and reads as a cheek patch instead of a nose. **When a model looks wrong but every measurement checks out, the answer is the pose, not the numbers** — and an axis-aligned box system needs one optional rotation to have any pose at all.
- **Two species sharing a model is right; sharing a size is not.** Horse, mule and donkey are one net layout and one box list, which is correct and is how the original does it. They are *not* one size — the original scales the whole model to 0.92 and 0.87. Rendering all three identically is a bug the user spotted immediately ("i know that the donkeys tend to be smaller than horses"). The scale multiplies geometry and placement only and must never touch the UV rectangles.
- **A subject with 25 colours does not want flat fields *or* dithering, and getting that wrong looks like coloured paper.** The first draft of the nine skins authored one tone per face and measured 9–13 colours against references at 19–36. Every anatomical decision was right and it still looked like card. Almost all of a reference's colour count sits **one step either side of each face's base tone**, sprinkled — not spent on contrast. Three tools fixed it: weighted per-texel grain, a coverage *rate* so the grain perturbs a vignette instead of erasing it, and swatch-mixing to get the intermediate tones a five-tone coat cannot supply. See `TEXTURING.md` §4.
- **Standing a box net on end rotates its texture too, and for the rabbit that landed right rather than wrong.** Pitching the torso 85° nose-up puts the net's belly rect facing forward and its rump rect facing down, which is exactly how a rabbit sitting on its haunches is arranged — so check where each rect ends up before assuming a large rotation needs a re-authored net. The knock-on is that an upright animal is far taller than a level one built from the same boxes, so `modelScale` has to drop hard: the rabbit went 0.88 → 0.48 to stay rabbit-sized, and got the slimmer silhouette the user had asked for as a side effect.
- **Two nets of identical footprint are ambiguous by arithmetic and unambiguous by colour.** The frog's back and belly are both 7×9 and differ only in height, so reading them as head-and-body is arithmetically legal — and produces an animal twice as long as it is wide, which every measurement would still pass. Averaging the two side bands settled it in one command: patterned orange over flat pale tan is a back over a belly, so they **stack**. This is the third time colour has resolved what geometry could not (the cow's udder, the goat's ruff, now this), which is enough to call it the rule: **when two readings are equally consistent, sample the pixels rather than reasoning about anatomy.**
- **The whole "prove the model, then author the skin" sequence now has a written order, and following it took the wolf and frog to a correct render on the second attempt each.** `TEXTURING.md` "Reading a net you have never seen before" records it: alpha runs before islands, missing rects as placement clues, colour where arithmetic ties, a shared-extent scan before rendering, then two renders and the eye. The order matters because **every step's failure mode is invisible to the next one** — a wrong dimension, a wrong interpretation of correct dimensions, and a z-fighting shared extent all produce something that looks like a plausible animal in a still frame.
- **A missing top or bottom rect outranks any colour reading, and the frog proves it.** Its two body nets are both 7×9 and differ only in height, so §12's "sample the colour" was used to call (3,1) the back and (0,13) the belly. That was **backwards**, and it shipped a frog with a hole through its spine for two milestones. The decisive fact was in the alpha the whole time: **(3,1) has no top rect and (0,13) has no bottom rect, so they interlock and (0,13) must be the upper box.** Colour could not settle it because both *side* bands are patterned and neither says which way is up. An absent face is a statement about what covers it — read the absences first, and use colour only where every rect is present.
- **Read the whole model from the alpha before deciding what any one net is.** The polar bear's second-largest net is a 12×12×10 slab sitting exactly where a head would go — it is the **shoulder hump**, and the real head is a far smaller 7×7×7. Same trap as the goat's ruff, and the same tell: the piece that looks like a head leaves the actual head nowhere to sit. Its front legs are also 8 deep against the hind pair's 6, so building all four from one net would quietly lose the heavy front end that makes a bear a bear.
- **Size and z-fighting are different problems, and three rounds were spent trading one for the other.** The villager's crossed forearms meet the upper arms where the reference lines up *three* pairs of faces — undersides, fronts and backs. Minecraft never draws the inward side of a surface so it does not care; every creature quad of ours is double-sided, so all three fight. Each attempted fix resized the part — bigger bulged, equal flickered, smaller looked recessed — and **resizing cannot cure a coincident plane.** The answer is to match the cross-sections exactly and **offset the bridging box about a sixteenth of a texel along its own height and depth axes**, which separates the planes at a size difference of zero. `TEXTURING.md` §14.5b has the rule. **`growSide` was added for the same join**: `grow` swells every axis, so using it to reach toward a neighbour thickens the part as much as it lengthens it.
- **But "shares a rig" has to be proven over the *whole* sheet, and a truncated dump is worse than no dump.** The villager and zombie villager are identical row for row up to row 33 — head, nose, body, legs, every net — so a listing cut off at row 30 said "same model" with total confidence. They differ in exactly one dimension, visible in exactly four rows: the villager's arm side rows stop at 33 (a 4×8×4 upper arm plus a separate crossbar for the folded pose) and the zombie villager's run to 37 (a 4×12×4 zombie-length arm, no crossbar). The zombie villager shipped with arms a third too short, and **the user caught it by eye while apologising for not knowing the game** — which is the whole argument for handing them the build. Two rules: never `Select-Object -First` a run dump you are deriving from, and when two sheets look identical, the interesting difference is wherever one of them *stops*.
- **A whole-sheet alpha diff produces false negatives on rig sharing, so it is a hint and never a verdict.** The Blackbone (wither skeleton), stray and bogged all report as "differing" from the skeleton because their interior pixels differ — ribcage holes, a skull's eye sockets, the bogged's mushrooms. Their **net boundaries are identical**, which is the only thing that decides the model. Compare run *widths at the net edges*, not opaque pixel counts.
- **A face mapping can be wrong for thirty species and invisible in every one of them.** `uprightBox` mapped its **top and bottom rects backwards front-to-back** from the day it was written. A box net folds so the top rect's *last* row meets the front face, and the code had the first row there. Nothing caught it because every model up to that point had top and bottom rects that are near enough symmetric — a sheep's crown, a pig's back, a villager's scalp. It surfaced the moment a model arrived with a **stripe painted down the front of its back**, which rendered at the rear; the user spotted it in one screenshot. **When a shared mapping has never been exercised asymmetrically, it has never been tested** — and the fix is to the one function, not to the model that exposed it.
- **Do not build a mob's model and its skin in the same pass, and do not assume a species is wanted just because it was asked for.** Ten mobs were added in one session and one of them — the hoglin — was removed entirely a day later, model, nets, docs and all. That cost nothing, because every one of them was a net table plus a species row plus a rig reused from something else. **The cheapness is the point:** a species that is one table row is a species you can delete. The moment a mob needs bespoke movement code, that stops being true, so keep new creatures on existing rigs unless the reference genuinely demands otherwise.

- **"Which mobs have arms" is not the same question as "which mobs punch", and shipping the first cost a correction.** The attack swing was rolled out by *rig*, so every skeleton on the biped rig mimed a sword blow. They are **archers** — the reference gives them `ranged_attack` at priority 0 and only drops one to melee when it has no bow — and the user's objection was immediate: _"remove the skeleton's arms moving, they use bow and arrow not melee attack, think about the game properly."_ They were right, and the fix is a species field rather than a rig flag, because the Blackbone shares that exact rig and *does* swing: it carries a stone sword. **A rig is geometry and an attack is behaviour; the moment an animation is chosen by the first, it will be wrong for whichever species shares a body and not a job.**
- **A refactor whose acceptance test is "nothing changed" wants a mechanism that *cannot* change the untouched cases, not merely care.** M20c had to move retaliation, the grudge, `huntsBelowLight` and herd alerting out of branches and into table rows without altering any of them, and rendering a head turn meant editing `buildMesh` \u2014 a thousand lines of art the user had already signed off. The trick that made it safe was **swapping two local vectors rather than touching any box builder**: `forward` and `side` became mutable, `headParts(true/false)` points them at the head's yaw, and every existing `place`/`uprightBox`/`lyingBox` call picks the change up for free. A species that never calls it emits **byte-identical geometry**, so the roster could be converted one animal at a time with no possibility of regressing the rest. Reach for "the untouched path is provably unchanged" before reaching for care.
- **A creature's facial features are placed against a *measured* landmark, not by eye, and the llama proves why.** Its muzzle sat centred at 2.02 against a neck box whose top is 2.0125 — so it covered the face entirely, and the user's report was that "the nose is wayyy above its eyes". The fix came from two numbers off the texture rather than from nudging: the eyes are painted on the neck net's **front rect at rows 1-2 below its top**, and the reference muzzle spans **rows 2-6**, so the muzzle's top edge meets the underside of the eyes and everything follows. **When a face looks wrong, find where the eyes are painted on the net and place everything else relative to that** - it is one command and it turns a guess into arithmetic.
- **An overlay is not a skin, and the machinery for one already existed.** The charged Bramble's energy shell needed no shader work, no new texture layer and no tint: creature skins are *already* alpha-tested cutouts, so a mostly-transparent overlay net drawn as a second, slightly larger shell just works. The sheep's fleece has done exactly this since M19. **Before adding a rendering path, check whether an existing asset arrangement already produces the effect.**

- **A refactor that must not change 36 things is safe only if the untouched path is *provably* unchanged, and "at zero it reduces to the old call" is the cheapest form of that proof.** Converting every limb from a translated box to a joint rotation touched a thousand lines of signed-off model work. What made it survivable was that `legBox` is **additive**: `uprightBox` kept its exact signature and maths, and `legBox` at an angle of zero emits precisely the box `uprightBox` would have. So an unconverted species is byte-identical by construction rather than by care, and the roster converts one animal at a time. Same property that let `headParts()` roll out at M20c. Reach for it before reaching for diligence.
- **Two boxes can be the right sizes, the right UVs and in each other's places, and every metric still passes.** The camel's `7x8x19` is its *neck* - a long low bar running forward from the chest - and its `7x14x7` is the *head* standing on that bar's far end. They shipped swapped, which put the tall box back at the shoulder where it read as a slab bolted onto the animal; the user's words were "its neck is an extra block which i don't think should be there". Nothing in `TEXTURING.md` \u00a713 catches this, because the net table records sizes and origins and both were correct. **`Mojang/bedrock-samples` settles it in one fetch** - `resource_pack/models/entity/<mob>.geo.json` gives the cube origins, so *where* each box goes is arithmetic rather than judgement. Fetch it before deriving placement by eye, not after a bug report.
- **The internet is a resource; working numbers out from first principles when someone has already published them is wasted time.** Direct user instruction, 2026-08-04: _"instead of working out numbers, remember the internet is a resource? search it up and fix it."_ I had invented plausible ore rarities and drop counts by reasoning about what felt balanced. The wiki has them exactly \u2014 copper drops 2\u20135, redstone 4\u20135, lapis 4\u20139, everything else 1 \u2014 along with the tier each ore demands. **The same instruction caught a second error in the same session:** reach had been 12 m since M7, which is Bedrock's *touch creative* figure. The mouse values are 5 m for blocks and 3 m for entities. Nobody would ever have found that by deriving; it took one page fetch. **Fetch before deriving, and when a plausible number has no source, say so rather than letting it settle.**
- **Two boxes that merely *touch* z-fight exactly as overlapping ones do, and §14.5's scan does not catch it.** That scan looks for shared extents among *overlapping* boxes. A turtle's head resting on the top plane of its shell, or a dolphin's head meeting its body at identical 8×7 cross-sections, overlaps in nothing at all and still puts two double-sided quads on one plane. Same cure as §14.5b: overlap them slightly with different `grow` values rather than letting the faces meet. **The general form of this rule is now settled — a coincident plane is a coincident plane however the boxes arrived at it.**
- **The "reduces to the old behaviour at its default" property has now made three risky rollouts safe, and it should be reached for before diligence.** `legBox` at angle zero emits exactly `uprightBox`'s box; `beginHead` at rest reduces to the old expression; and `walkTo` with no route steers with the identical fan it replaced. In each case the untouched cases are unchanged **by construction** rather than by care, which is what allows a signed-off roster of thirty-six models or a four-milestone-old movement system to be converted one piece at a time.
- **A whole-sheet alpha diff is a hint about rig sharing; the geometry file is the verdict.** `tools/compare-nets.ps1` reports the skeleton horse as differing from the horse on **51 of 64 rows** and they share the rig exactly — the differences are skeletal holes in the artwork, not net boundaries. `Mojang/bedrock-samples`' `<mob>.entity.json` names the geometry outright and settles it in one fetch: the zombified piglin and the piglin brute are both `geometry.piglin`, the trader llama is `geometry.llama.v1.8` plus a decor overlay. **Fetch the entity file before deriving anything from pixels.**
- **A mob's identifying feature is not always in its skin.** The mooshroom's alpha matches the cow's on every row, and `geometry.mooshroom.v2` has no mushroom cubes at all — they are separate **block** models placed by a render controller. So a mushroom cow built from the skin alone is a red cow, and shipping one would be exactly the "compiled and looked plausible but was subtly wrong" failure this file is full of. Check the geometry for what the *name* promises before assuming a skin swap covers it.
- **A pair of limbs shares one net, so drawing both sides identically puts the artwork outward on one and inward on the other.** Our `+side` face reads the rect at `u` and the `-side` face the one at `u + netD + netW`, which means a limb placed at a negative side offset shows the *far* flap outward. This was wrong for every quadruped from the beginning and stayed invisible for thirty-six species, because leg textures are almost always near-symmetric — until the skeleton horse, which paints bone on the first flap and leaves the second bare, so one flank wore its bones on the inside. The reference's answer is a `mirror` flag and so is ours. **The remaining models are still unmirrored: the same latent bug is waiting for the next asymmetric skin.**
- **A coincident plane can hide for the entire roster and then be exposed by transparency rather than by geometry.** Every quadruped here puts a leg's top face on exactly the body's underside — the horse's are both `0.6875` to the last digit — and it never showed, because both surfaces are opaque and much the same colour so whichever quad won looked identical. The **skeleton** horse's underside is barely a third painted, so the fight became visible *through the gaps*. **Two boxes that merely touch z-fight exactly as overlapping ones do, and neither §14.5's shared-extent scan nor a still screenshot of an opaque animal will find it.** The cure is the usual one: overlap by a few thousandths, never touch. Worth knowing that a `grow` cancels out of `legBox`'s `hang`, so it moves the extents without moving the pivot.

## Animation and rigging

- **The convenient upload helper was the bottleneck, and it was written that way on purpose at M9.** `uploadBufferData` allocates a staging buffer, submits, and calls `vkQueueWaitIdle` — per buffer. That was the right call when one mesh existed; by M12 it meant 726 full GPU stalls at startup and two per block break, and it had quietly become the largest cost in the frame. **A helper documented as "fine at load time and nowhere else" will end up everywhere unless something forces the issue.** Batching into one arena, one submit and one fence took it from 186 ms to 33 ms. Check whether the slow-but-simple path from an early milestone is still confined to where it was meant to be.
- **The same bounce came back for dropped items, from a different mistake with the same shape.** Drops were rested a small `kRestHeight` *above* the surface to make them hover. That leaves a gap they immediately fall through, and landing pushes them back up — a loop again. **Anything that rests on a surface must rest exactly on it**; visual hover belongs in the animation, not the physics. The related trap is the ground probe: `floor(bottom)` points at the *air* cell whenever the object sits exactly on a boundary, so it has to probe slightly below.
- **A suppression flag that is only set on the frame an action fires does not suppress anything afterwards.** `struckCreature` was true only on the frame a swing landed, but the swing then locked out for 0.45 s — during which the dig branch saw `false` and happily mined the block behind the animal. The question "is a creature in the way?" has to be asked **every frame, independently of whether the swing is ready**, because it is a fact about the world and not an outcome of the action. Splitting the ray query out of `strike` made the two questions separate.
- **One gait for every species makes a camel mince and a chicken plod.** The leg cycle was a single hardcoded `gait += distance * 6` with one amplitude. How fast the legs cycle per metre travelled is a property of the animal, not of the engine, and it belongs in the species row beside its speed. A rabbit needed more than a number: it *hops*, so it raises the whole model on an arc instead of swinging legs — which means the lift has to reach every box, not just the ones that look like legs.
- **The zombie's arm swing and the humanoid's run in opposite directions, and it is not a sign slip.** `animation.zombie.attack_bare_hand` is `sin(tπ)·1.2 − sin((1−(1−t)²)π)·0.4` **added** to an arms-out pitch, so arms already held in front chop *down*; `animation.humanoid.attack.rotations` is `sin((1−(1−t)³)π)` **subtracted** from a hanging pitch, so a weapon arm swings *up*. Fetching `resource_pack/animations/zombie.animation.json` settled it in one call, and the same file confirmed the idle sway already in the code — its `2.865` and `103.13` are exactly our 0.05 rad on a 3.5 s period. **The shipped animation also uses a flat −90° base rather than the −120°/−80° aggressive-versus-calm split I recalled**, so the arms are *not* raised on sighting you. That difference is the whole argument for fetching rather than recalling.
- **A curve that is zero at both ends needs no state flag.** Both swing curves return exactly zero at phase 0 and phase 1, so "is this creature swinging" is already answered by a timer that has run out — no branch to get wrong, and no pose to reset.
- **Rotating a limb about its own centre swings the root into the body.** The chicken's wings are hinged at the shoulder, not through the middle, which is one extra line: place the box's centre by rotating its offset *from the pivot* rather than passing the resting centre. Centre-rotation is fine for something small and symmetric and obviously wrong for anything that hangs off a body.

- **"The top of the limb does not move" is wrong, and it is the trap that opens a wedge at the hip.** Only the pivot *point* is fixed; the top **face** tilts, and its corners swing by half the in-plane extent times `sin(angle)`. The reference's answer is an **overhang** - a biped arm pivots 2 texels below its top so that much box stays buried in the torso through the whole swing (`ANIMATION.md` §1.2 names it explicitly). Derive it from the box (`min(netW, netD)/2` reproduces both the arm and the chicken's wing); never author it. **The hip is where a rotation's sweep concentrates**, so §14.5's shared-extent check belongs there first, and at the swing extremes rather than at rest.
- **The spider's walk is worth copying exactly rather than approximating with one sine.** Ours gave alternate pairs opposite phases off a single shared sine, so the legs rowed in two groups. `animation.spider.walk` uses **a quarter turn of phase per pair**, a fore-and-aft sweep at **twice** the rate of the lift, and **absolute values on both**, so a leg only ever moves to one side of its rest pose. That last part is what keeps the travel small. And the thing that stops adjacent tips colliding is not the sweep but the **lift**: two neighbours pass within half a degree of each other in yaw, and are several texels apart vertically exactly when they do. **Java takes the cosine signed and Bedrock does not** - a real edition difference, in a formula that otherwise matches term for term.
- **A limb is not always a leg, and "pivot at the top" is only one of two locations.** The spider's legs run *sideways* and hinge at their **inner end, mid-height**, so their swing is a **yaw** rather than a tilt - which is the head-turn mechanism, not the leg one. Treating them as an exception to skip was wrong; §1.2's pivot table lists them. The silverfish is the genuine exception, because it has no limb at all and its wriggle is a sideways *distance*.
- **Changing what a column *means* is the same hazard as widening what a value can be, and the compiler cannot see either.** `gaitSwing` went from metres of foot travel to radians of joint rotation. Two species were using it as a distance for something that is not a leg - the silverfish's wriggle and the spider's scuttle - and both would have compiled and rendered nonsense. Grep for every reader before repurposing a field, exactly as when widening a block's shape.
- **The reference's constants are calibrated to the reference's speeds.** Minecraft saturates its limb amplitude at 5 m/s because its mobs move at 4 m/s; ours walk at 1.6. Copying the 5 verbatim would have left the whole roster barely lifting a foot. Saturating against each species' **own `runSpeed`** lands the walk/run ratio at a consistent ~0.45 across all 36 rows for free, because those two numbers were tuned together. **Ask what a reference constant is a fraction *of* before porting it.**
- **Per-species rates are a decision, not an accident, and the reference having one number is not an argument.** §2.2 calibrates Minecraft at a single 2.665 rad/m; the user's standing call is that per-species rate stays, because one gait for everything makes a camel mince and a chicken plod. Amplitude got the same treatment - the roster's hand-tuned spread was carried across rather than flattened to the reference's single value. **Say which knobs you are touching before touching them.**
- **A rotation about the wrong origin looks like the part is *sliding*, not turning, and it took a playtest to name it.** The head turn shipped at M20c as a whole-frame axis swap, which rotates about the **creature's own origin** - so every head placed forward of centre *orbited*: it swung out sideways and lunged forward as it looked, and dragged the neck along with it. The user's words were "sticking the head out and then looking - it's creepy". The maths was not wrong so much as pivotless, and it was invisible for as long as nobody watched a creature turn its head while standing still. **Any "rotate this group" needs its pivot named at the same moment the rotation is written**, or the origin silently becomes the pivot. The fix also delivered head *pitch* for free, because a pivot was the only thing pitch had been waiting for.
- **Splitting one boolean-flag function into two named ones turns a silent omission into a compile error.** `headParts(bool)` became `beginHead(neckForward, neckUp)` and `endHead()`. There is no default pivot, so a head group that forgets one does not build - which matters at 23 call sites where the wrong answer is a head hovering in the wrong place rather than anything that throws. Prefer this to a defaulted parameter whenever the default would be *wrong* rather than merely unset.
- **Where the reference parents a part is a decision we are allowed to overrule, and here we did.** Mojang keep the camel's neck inside the head bone, so a camel there swings its whole neck to look. Over a 19-texel neck at our 52\u00b0 clamp that is a lunge, and the eyes are on the head box anyway - so ours leaves the neck with the body. Direct user instruction: _"only the head should be turning on the camel."_ Written at the call site so a later session does not "correct" it back to the reference.

- **A hand-tuned offset outlives the mechanism it was tuned for, and the compiler cannot see it.** The zombie and husk arms sat at a rest height of 1.42 - a number that made sense when a limb was a *translated* box, and became wrong the moment `legBox` started **deriving the joint from the rest centre**. It put the shoulder at 1.68 against a head spanning 1.5 to 2.0, so the arms hinged from the ears. Every other limb on the roster converted safely because `legBox` at angle zero reproduces `uprightBox` exactly - but these two are the only limbs posed at 90°, where the pivot is the whole of what the pose depends on. **After changing what a value is measured from, the rows to re-check are the ones that were compensating for the old measurement**, and they are exactly the rows that look most deliberately tuned.
- **A symmetric sine is a walk cycle, and using one for anything that is not walking will be spotted immediately.** The squid's first tentacle animation was `sin(t)`, and the user's word for it was that the squid was *walking*. The reference's curve is `sin(f²π)` over the first half of the cycle: it flares **slowly** and snaps shut **fast**, and the asymmetry is the entire read. Crucially the **thrust is applied during the snap only**, from the same number that drives the pose, so the push and the picture cannot drift apart — and a consequence falls out for free that would have been hard to author, namely that a squid must turn about before it can go the other way, because it does not steer while it is pushing.
- **A box whose rect is entirely transparent is not free — it is noise.** The skeleton horse paints nothing at all in the tail rect and only two one-pixel columns in the mane, so those boxes contributed stray slivers rather than a tail. Measure coverage per rect when a skin is mostly holes; "it renders nothing" and "it renders almost nothing" look very different on screen.

## Vulkan and the renderer

- **Backface culling errors are completely silent, and a hand derivation of the winding was wrong.** With `VK_CULL_MODE_BACK_BIT`, getting `frontFace` backwards keeps the far half of every closed object and discards the near half — boxes render as hollow shells you are looking *into*. Validation layers say nothing, the frame rate is unchanged, and every individual triangle is drawn correctly. Only a human looking at a closed shape catches it. On 2026-07-30 the value was derived by hand — twice, through the Vulkan signed-area formula — reasoning that flipping Y in the projection reverses screen winding, concluding `VK_FRONT_FACE_CLOCKWISE`. **That was wrong; the correct value with this codebase's CCW-from-outside geometry and Y-flipped projection is `VK_FRONT_FACE_COUNTER_CLOCKWISE`,** established by looking at the screen. The derivation's flaw was never found. **Do not re-derive this and "fix" it — verify against a closed box viewed from outside.**
- **Sharing vertices between cube faces destroys the edges that make it read as a cube.** The first cube used 8 shared corners; where three faces meet they were forced to share one colour, so the GPU blended smoothly across every edge and the result looked like a soft blob. Geometrically perfect, visually unreadable. The fix is **4 vertices per face, 24 per box** — which is what voxel meshing needs regardless, since normals and texture coordinates also differ per face at a shared corner. A vertex is position *plus everything that varies*; only the position is actually shared.
- **A Windows console window freezes the program the moment you click inside it.** Cost real confusion on 2026-07-30: the Vulkan SDK installer was reported as "a blank TUI" doing nothing. It was not broken — the window title had become `Select C:\...\vulkansdk-...exe`, and that `Select ` prefix is Windows' QuickEdit mode, entered by a single click in the console. In that mode the process blocks the next time it writes to stdout. **Diagnosis:** check `(Get-Process <name>).MainWindowTitle` for a `Select ` prefix and compare `.CPU` seconds against elapsed wall time — a process using 0.08 CPU-seconds over 90 seconds is parked, not working. **Fix:** press `Esc` or `Enter` in that window. Applies to any long-running console tool, not just installers.
- **`std::this_thread::sleep_for` is far too coarse for frame pacing on Windows.** The default system timer resolution is about 15.6 ms, so asking to sleep 8.3 ms for a 120 fps cap actually sleeps ~15.6 ms and lands near 64 fps. The naive implementation would have looked like a mysterious "my cap doesn't work" bug. `FrameLimiter` instead uses a Win32 **high-resolution waitable timer** (`CreateWaitableTimerExW` with `CREATE_WAITABLE_TIMER_HIGH_RESOLUTION`) and spins the final ~500 µs. `timeBeginPeriod(1)` was rejected because it changes timer resolution process-wide as a side effect. Measured result: a steady 120–121 fps against a 120 target. Reuse this pattern for any future precise wait; do not reach for `sleep_for`.
- **PowerShell prints a native program's stderr as a red `NativeCommandError`, which is not a failure.** `cl.exe`, `code --install-extension`, and `vulkaninfo` all write informational banners and warnings to stderr and still succeed. Check the exit code and the actual text before reporting a problem.
- **Opaque artwork cannot be made translucent by turning on blending.** The hotbar slots stayed solid black through a working alpha pipeline, because the sprite's interior pixels are opaque black in the source image — blending faithfully blended *black at full alpha*. The fix was not a shader or blend-state change but a geometry one: draw the frame as four edge strips and simply never draw the interior. **Check what the source pixels actually contain before debugging the blend path.**
- **A feature can be "finished" and never actually run, and only rendering it exposes that.** M11 was signed off with `RenderStats` reporting GPU time and draw calls. Both were dead: `m_frameDrawCalls` was incremented but never copied into `m_stats` or reset, and **no `vkCmdWriteTimestamp` was ever recorded at all**, so `m_timestampsPending` stayed false and the read path returned early forever. Everything compiled, validation was silent, and the console log never printed either figure. The bug surfaced the instant the numbers appeared on screen as `gpu 0.00 ms` and `draws 0`. **A statistic that is only ever stored is untested; display it or assert on it.**
- **Never benchmark threading on a Debug build.** M12 first measured as a *regression*: 813 ms single-threaded before, 1325 ms with eleven workers after. The same code in Release went 179 ms → 55 ms. MSVC's debug CRT serialises the heap, so eleven threads allocating mesh vectors spend their time queueing on one lock, and the debug numbers were not merely noisy but **non-monotonic** — four workers beat eleven. Two rounds of "optimisation" were spent chasing that phantom before building Release. Debug is for validation layers; Release is for numbers.
- **Blending is a draw-order problem, not a shader problem.** Water looked correct in isolation and wrong against terrain, because a translucent face blends with whatever is already in the framebuffer — and the seabed behind it had not been drawn yet. No amount of tuning alpha fixes that. Chunks now carry two meshes and every opaque one is drawn before any translucent one. **If transparency looks wrong, check what order it is drawn in before touching the blend state.**
- **A placeholder can quietly break a feature three milestones later, in a system it has no obvious connection to.** M14c multiplied every lit surface by the sun's ambient-plus-directional term. That is correct for sunlight and wrong for everything else, so a glowstone placed in a cave was multiplied by the daytime ambient of 0.22 and, on faces pointing away from the sun, lit nothing at all. The light system was working perfectly; the *shader* was overwriting its result. **Sky light and block light must stay separate all the way into the fragment shader** — they are packed into the vertex colour's red and green channels, which cost nothing because the shading was greyscale and blue was spare. When a lighting bug appears, check what the shader does to the value before assuming the propagation is wrong.
- **`discard` is not free of device features.** Compiled against Vulkan 1.3 it becomes `OpDemoteToHelperInvocation`, which validation rejects unless `shaderDemoteToHelperInvocation` is enabled. Release builds ran fine because validation is off there — which is exactly why the debug build has to be run before a milestone is called done, not just built.
- **Two boxes of the same width will z-fight wherever they overlap, and every creature quad is double-sided so nothing hides it.** The camel's eyes are painted across the two front corners of one box, and the box in front of it happens to be the same 7 texels across — so their side faces landed on exactly one plane, right over the eyes, and the winner flipped per pixel and per frame. The user described it as triangles forming over the eyes, which is the tell: z-fighting exposes a quad's diagonal, because the two triangles interpolate depth slightly differently. **Grow the box carrying the artwork rather than insetting the one in front** — insetting cascades, and shrinking the camel's face box moved its top and bottom to within a few thousandths of the ears and the muzzle, merely relocating the problem. The rule for this model system: overlapping boxes must differ in extent on every shared axis, and the one with the detail on it wins.
- **Convert the reference's per-tick probabilities, or the feature scales with frame rate.** Bedrock's `look_at_player` rolls 0.02 **per tick**, and a tick is a fixed twentieth of a second. Our tick is a frame, so used verbatim a chicken at 120 fps would glance six times as often as one at 20 \u2014 a bug that only appears on someone else's machine. The conversion is `0.02 / 0.05` as a per-second rate, the same family as the velocity \u00d720 and acceleration \u00d7400 in `RESEARCH.md` \u00a71.2.

- **Per-frame sampling of a per-tick decision needs a memory, not just a conversion.** The reference tests a creeper's line of sight twenty times a second; we test it every frame, so at 120 fps a fence post clipped for one frame is six times likelier to be caught, and every catch reverses the fuse. A single tick of grace (`kSightGrace`) restores the reference's sampling rate. Same family as converting `look_at_player`'s per-tick probability, and worth checking for wherever a boolean drives a countdown.

- **A tint is not fog, and the difference is the whole look.** Ours was a full-screen quad at 42% alpha: it announced the state and said nothing about distance, so an underwater scene had no depth at all. Real distance fog needed one varying (`gl_Position.w` is already the view depth under a perspective projection, so it is free), one push constant, and a `mix` - far less than the flat quad implied it would cost.
- **`MeshPushConstants` is now 128 bytes, which is Vulkan's guaranteed minimum.** A `mat4` plus four `vec4`s exactly fills it. The next thing that wants a push constant cannot just add one - it needs a descriptor set, or a check against the device's real limit. **AMD really does expose only 128**, so this is not theoretical, and the Steam Deck is on the list of things this project wants to keep possible. `animation.zw` is the only spare room left.
- **A colour that is "the value from the reference, times something" is not that colour, and the user will notice before you do.** The underwater fog was quoted as `#1165b0` and then multiplied by the daylight term on the way to the shader - so it was only ever the stated colour at noon and drifted to a murky navy the rest of the day. The reference's fog data carries a flat constant with no light modulation at all; the multiply was invented, unfaithful *and* the thing that looked wrong. **When a value is taken from a source, ship it unmodified unless the source modifies it.**
- **A colour in shipped data is an *input* to the renderer, not a pixel, and the fog colour was wrong for a third time because of it.** With the daylight multiply gone, `#1165b0` straight out of `ocean_fog_setting.json` still read as "a weird blue" - because Minecraft blends that value with the water's own colour before anything reaches the screen, so the raw number never appears anywhere. **Measuring the user's own screenshots settled it in one command and the failure was a single channel**: fully-fogged ocean is `#146FFF` = rgb(20, 111, 255) - one exact colour across 24,644 sampled pixels - and a deeper scene `#1762CD` = rgb(23, 98, 205). Red and green matched the JSON almost exactly; **only blue was off, 176 against 205-255**, which is exactly what "not blue enough, looks grey" means. Ours is now the measured middle, `#1465E7`. **A constant lifted from data is not verified until something rendered from it has been compared against a picture** - and `SYSTEM_MEMORY.md` now carries a do-not-revert box, because "the JSON says 0x1165b0" is a very convincing-looking correction to make.

## Textures, atlases and sprites

- **Smooth noise is the wrong tool for pixel art, and "quantize it afterwards" does not rescue it.** The first two attempts at block textures used smoothstep-interpolated value noise mapped through a palette. The result was soft melted blobs that the user called "odd and uncanny" — correctly, because interpolation *is* blurring, and blurring is the one thing pixel art never does. Real block textures are per-pixel choices from a **narrow** palette, weighted so most pixels sit on middle tones, with sparse accent pixels (the grey pebbles in soil) and short horizontal runs. Contrast instinct is also wrong: reference stone spans about five near-identical greys, roughly a third of the range that felt right. **When making art, get reference in front of you before generating anything** — two rewrites were spent guessing at what "looks like a block texture" means.
- **Measure the reference's statistics instead of describing it in words.** "Bark is vertical stripes" produced whole 16-pixel columns painted one tone, which the user correctly called "all lines and stuff" — it reads as planks. Counting run lengths settled it in one command: the reference's vertical runs are overwhelmingly **1-3 pixels** and 90% of horizontal runs are a single pixel, so bark is a field of short dashes where neighbouring pixels almost never match. Three further numbers each caught a separate mistake: column means showed one column at luminance 59 against another at 122 (the "big fat strip of dark"), which came from applying a per-column tone lean to *every* run rather than letting each column draw from a narrow window; consecutive runs picking the same tone silently merged into chunky 5-9px slabs; and tiling the texture out 3×2 showed that a strict `x % 2` light/dark alternation makes the 16-pixel repeat obvious, where the reference runs two dark or two light columns together often enough to hide it. **Tile a texture before judging it** — a seam or a repeat is invisible in a single tile.
- **The same "tight value range" rule applies to detail, and I broke it twice in one texture set.** Log end-grain rings were given a 35-luminance jump where the reference uses **7**, and the bark border was two pixels where the reference is one. Rings are thin 1px lines on a light field, not thick alternating bands.
- **The agent is the artist, and the exception has grown — but only in *who authors*, never in *where reference art may live*.** Stated plainly by the user, 2026-07-30: _"buddy there is no artist, you ARE the artist. this is a solo dev project."_ That still holds for block textures, HUD art, the font and item icons — do not defer those, do not ship deliberately rough placeholders and call them someone else's problem, and do not treat "I can't see the screen" as an excuse; magnify the output and look at it (`tools/preview-textures.ps1` exists for exactly this).

  **It no longer holds for creature skins.** On 2026-08-03 the user asked an artist friend to take them on. Two phases sit ahead of her and both are slow: **researching how UV texturing works** — how a flat image wraps onto a 3D model — and then **drawing the art**, which is the longer half. Weeks, not days. **Do not re-author the creature skins to "help", and do not treat the wait as a problem to route around.** The generator that produced our rejected attempt still exists as a fallback and as living documentation of the nets; leave it alone unless asked. What *is* useful is keeping `TEXTURING.md` §13 and §14 accurate, because those are what she works from.

  **Almost every texture in the running build is currently Mojang's, and that is deliberate, sanctioned and temporary.** Direct user decision, 2026-08-02: _"yeah just replace all 9 textures with mojang's, i'll have my friend rework them."_ Four staged sets sit **beside each `game.exe`, never in `assets/`** — creature skins, spawn eggs, catalogue tabs and every block and item texture. Nothing is being smuggled anywhere. A future session finding this **should not panic, should not rip it out, and should not lecture the user about it**; the only hard requirement is that none of it survives into a release build. `assets/textures/hud.png` is the one exception that does live under `assets/`, also at the user's request.
- **A raised bevel is asymmetric, so a sprite's centre is not its interior's centre.** The hotbar cell has a three-pixel bevel on the top and left but two on the bottom and right — that asymmetry *is* the raised look. Centring icons on the tile put them visibly high and left. Anything placed inside a bevelled frame must be positioned from the interior rectangle, not the outer one.
- **`add_custom_command(TARGET ... POST_BUILD)` does not run when nothing was rebuilt, so editing only an asset silently shipped the old one.** The CMake file carried a comment claiming the opposite — that copying after every build meant "editing a texture and rebuilding is enough to see the change" — and that comment was wrong from the day it was written. Ninja reports `no work to do`, the copy never fires, and the game loads the previous texture while you stare at it wondering why your edit did nothing. It is now a real target with `ALL` plus `copy_directory_if_different`. **A build step that only runs as a side effect of another step is not a build step**, and this class of bug wastes time specifically when iterating on art, which is when you are least inclined to suspect the build.
- **An alpha-tested pixel must be written fully opaque, and the failure looks like a lighting bug.** Cutout leaves came out pale grey at distance while near ones were fine. Mip levels average alpha, so distant foliage arrives at around 0.6 — above the `< 0.5` discard threshold, so it survives, and then blends with the sky because blending is enabled for every draw. **The diagnosis came from sampling the pixels rather than theorising:** the pale colour was almost exactly halfway between leaf green and sky blue, which named the mechanism immediately. Texture alpha is consumed by the discard test and must not reach the output; world transparency belongs in the vertex alpha, where water already keeps it.
- **Matching a reference statistic is not the same as looking right, and chasing the number shipped something visibly worse.** M19a rebuilt every block texture against measured reference statistics — palette size, luminance spread, run lengths — and most of that was a genuine improvement. Then it went further and copied the *mechanic* of biome-tinted foliage: author grass and leaves greyscale, multiply by a per-column colour. Every measurement agreed with the reference. The result was a world of pale sage-grey plants that the user called ugly on sight, and they were right. **The mechanism is arithmetic anyone could have predicted: a mid-grey texture times a mid-green tint lands far darker and duller than either, so authoring the texture bright enough to survive the multiply is a tuning problem that was never done.** Two rules follow. **Look at the result before calling it done** — a statistic moving toward the reference is evidence, not proof, and the screenshot took ten seconds to check and was never taken. And **the reference documents what Minecraft does, not what this game should do**; copying a mechanic needs its own justification, because the tint only pays for itself once there are enough biomes to notice, and it cost a vertex attribute, per-chunk climate storage and a merge-comparison field to deliver washed-out grass. All of it was reverted; the texture statistics work was kept.
- **PowerShell binds `,` tighter than arithmetic, and the failure lands at runtime.** `@(@($i, 0), @($i, $size - 1), ...)` parses as `(... , @($i, $size)) - 1`, which throws `[System.Object[]] does not contain a method named 'op_Subtraction'` once per call. Parenthesise the arithmetic, or better, avoid nested coordinate arrays entirely and write the four cases out. **The error was invisible because the run was filtered** with `Select-String "error|Exception"` and the message contains neither word — it says "Method invocation failed". The textures were written *without their border* and the script reported success. **When a generator's output looks unchanged, run it unfiltered before assuming the code is wrong.**
- **"You are the artist" is about not deferring art, not about when to author it — and I got that wrong on the UI.** Slice 3 of the inventory screen exists to de-risk the *layout*, and I drew original tab sprites for it. The user's reaction was immediate: _"who asked you to make textures? i thought instructions in mds were clear."_ They were. The standing instruction is theirs, from 2026-08-02 and stated generally: _"the reason i ask for mojang's textures is so that we know it works, and then after we use mojang's textures in the reference folder, we can develop our own."_ Twenty-one UI crops had been extracted into `reference/ui-icons/` for exactly this, and the brief said **"Measure from them."** I took the *numbers* from them and then drew my own pixels — which leaves two unknowns behind one symptom, because a tab that looks wrong could be bad geometry or bad art. **Stage the reference, prove the layout, author afterwards.** `tools/make-reference-hud.ps1` now does for the HUD what the creature atlas does for skins, with the same `assets/` guard.
- **A proof atlas must never resize what it composites, and the size check is the proof.** `make-reference-hud.ps1` throws if a tab crop is not exactly 22×25, because that dimension *is* the evidence that `crafting-ui.avif` is vanilla's layout at a clean 3×. Rescaling to fit would hide a drifted extraction behind a plausible-looking result — the same class of silent failure as the sheet-height bug, where a wrong size did not fail but slid every UV.
- **When our own art comes out wrong, read the reference again and rewrite it — do not tweak.** Direct user instruction, 2026-08-02: _"if the first iteration of our own texture doesn't work or looks ugly or whatever, instead of trying to tweak, just make it from scratch after looking at the reference texture AGAIN. if our texture is almost there and just needs a smidge of work then tweaking is fine."_ The reason it matters is that bad art is almost always the wrong **technique**, not the wrong **parameter**, and no amount of parameter nudging crosses that gap — the cow's patches needed grown blobs rather than per-cell odds, and the pig needed flat per-face planes rather than per-pixel palette picks. `tools/compare-skin.ps1` says which case you are in: **correlation near zero means rewrite**, because there is no structure to preserve. The pig went from -0.03 to 0.82 by being rewritten and 0.82 to 0.83 by being tuned.
- **Markings made from independent per-cell odds read as static, not as an animal.** A cow's patches were generated by rolling a 3×3 grid cell by cell; the user's verdict was "this looks like a cyborg", and that is exactly what independent rolls produce. Real markings are *connected blobs* three to eight texels across with ragged edges, made by placing a few centres and growing outward — nine stamped discs beat two hundred cell rolls. The same texture also had a bone-white muzzle where the reference lifts it only from luminance 56 to 82; **an accent is usually far subtler than instinct suggests**, and overdoing it turns a face into a mask.
- **A measurement can be wrong in a way that looks like the art being wrong, and it will not tell you which.** `tools/compare-skin.ps1` reported the cow's patches at 11.6× the reference's — damning — while the texture was actually fine. The fault was a **median threshold**, which only separates light from dark when the two are balanced; our markings cover about a third, so the median landed inside the hide and carved it up by its own faint variation into phantom patches. Otsu's method scored the identical texture at 0.9×. **Before acting on a metric, check its assumptions hold for the thing being measured** — and make it refuse to answer where they do not, which is why the tool now skips patch analysis on near-uniform or densely dithered references rather than emitting a confident wrong number.
- **A blunt regex over a script hits the definition as well as the call.** `-replace "New-CharcoalTexture", "New-CharcoalTexture`nNew-TorchTexture"` turned `function New-CharcoalTexture {` into a syntax error. Anchoring with `(?m)^…$` then silently matched nothing, because the files are CRLF. Edit script call-sites by hand, or match on something that only appears once.
- **PowerShell's `-File` flattens an array parameter into one string.** `powershell -File tools\preview-textures.ps1 -Files a.png,b.png` arrives as a single filename containing commas and throws "Missing file" listing all of them at once, which reads like a path problem rather than a quoting one. Dot-source or call the script directly (`& .\tools\preview-textures.ps1 -Files a,b`) when a parameter is `[string[]]`.
- **A current texture does not prove an old model map is still current.** The 26.2 chicken replaced its boxed legs with a one-texel strip and alpha-shaped foot, and the goat and rabbit repacked most appendages entirely. Legacy dimensions looked plausible and failed the alpha-footprint check. The reliable sequence is: look at the actual image, measure candidate nets against its alpha, check a compatible model source where one exists, then use clean original packing for any irregular islands rather than forcing an obsolete six-face net. `tools/analyze-alpha.ps1` and `tools/preview-grid.ps1` keep those checks visual and bounded.
- **Prove the model against the reference pixels first, then author the skin.** User instruction, 2026-08-02: _"the reason i ask for mojang's textures is so that we know it works, and then after we use mojang's textures in the reference folder, we can develop our own."_ A wrong net and a wrong skin produce the same symptom — pixels on the wrong face — so debugging both at once is guessing. `tools/make-reference-creature-atlas.ps1` packs the reference textures at our row offsets and writes `creatures-reference.png` **beside the exe**, never under `assets/`, and the game prefers it when present. That separation turned nine species from "looks wrong somehow" into a list of exact box dimensions in one pass. **Delete the atlas before any release** — it is the same category of exception as the HUD sheet, but with an expiry date.
- **Alpha runs per row beat a character grid, and a band narrower than the formula predicts is information about where the box is buried.** The §2 grid stops being readable past 64 columns, and the camel is 128. Printing each row's opaque runs makes the net arithmetic fall out directly: band rows are `2w` wide at `u+d`, full rows `2(w+d)` at `u`, and the two row counts are `d` and `h`. **Where a band is narrower than predicted, a rect is transparent — and *which* one tells you where the box sits.** The goat's ruff has an empty bottom rect, so it must be sunk into the body far enough that the hole never shows. The wolf's body shows 6 columns where 12 are predicted, and crucially at `u+d+w` rather than `u+d`, so it is the *top* rect that is missing — which after the torso's quarter turn points forward, which is where the mane must sit. That placed a second box before a line of code was written. **Never read an unexpected width as a measurement error; work out which rect is absent and ask what covers it.**
- **PowerShell variable names are case insensitive, so a local silently overwrote a parameter.** A mixing helper declared `[string]$B` and assigned the blue channel to `$b`. The int became the string `"186"`, `{2:X2}` printed it verbatim, and the six-character hex became seven — which read back as a completely different colour. Fifteen percent of the goat turned bright yellow and *every metric still looked reasonable*; only the palette dump showed it. Prefix locals in any function with single-letter parameters, and validate generated hex where it is consumed.
- **A two-stage generator with the sheet size written down twice will silently undo the first stage — and then poison everything downstream of it.** Growing `creatures.png` from 896 to 1088 rows worked in `make-creature-skins.ps1` and was thrown away by `make-roster-skins.ps1`, which carried its own `$sheetHeight = 896` and passed it to `New-Object Bitmap $loaded, $w, $h` — **the *scaling* constructor**. The sheet was resampled back down and saved, with no error and both scripts reporting success. **The second-order failure was worse and took longer to find.** The corrupted sheet was then composited into `creatures-reference.png`, which is what the game actually loads; fixing the sheet afterwards did *not* fix the atlas, so the sheep stayed mangled through a rebuild, a re-run and a verification pass that only ever checked the sheet. **Fixing a generator does not fix what was generated from it while it was broken — list the derived artifacts and rebuild every one.** `run.ps1` now rebuilds the atlas whenever the sheet is newer, `kCreatureSheetWidth/Height` in `Creature.hpp` is the single owner of the size, and `Main.cpp` reads the PNG header and logs an error on mismatch, because a wrong sheet height does not fail — it slides every UV and mistextures the entire roster in silence.
- **Two species can share a rig outright, and the alpha channel says so for free.** `ocelot.png` and `cat_tabby.png` have byte-identical opaque runs on every row, which is proof they are one model before any measuring is done — so the ocelot cost one lambda and a row offset. Diff the alpha of a new skin against every skin already modelled before deriving anything.
- **Skipping "decorative" alpha detail can be the difference between an animal and a box.** A spiky mane was written off as flat sprite noise on the sheet and left out; it was a clean box whose spikes are cut by alpha, and without it the animal was bald and the dark stripe on its back read as a texture error rather than as the root of a mane. **Parse an unexplained region as a net before calling it decoration** — the arithmetic takes one minute, and a scatter of loose bristles is exactly what alpha cutout looks like on a sheet.
- **A comment naming its own blocker outlives the blocker.** `blockTextureLayer`'s crafting-table branch said the reference's two-and-two side arrangement "needs a face direction the mesher does not supply". `FaceDirection` had supplied it since the furnace work, and the table had gone on returning its front on all four sides — the same shape as the `FurnaceSide` layer that existed and was never returned. **When you add the thing a comment says is missing, grep for the comment.**
- **Growing the creature sheet is safe only if every derived copy is rebuilt in order, and there are four of them.** `assets/textures/creatures.png` comes from `make-creature-skins.ps1` (which owns `$sheetHeight`), `creatures-reference.png` is composited *from that sheet* by the atlas tool, and the build then syncs both next to each of the two executables. Regenerate the sheet, **then** the atlas, then build - and check all four with a one-line size dump, because a stale one does not fail, it silently slides every UV. `kCreatureSheetWidth/Height` is the single owner and `Main.cpp` checks the PNG header against it.
- **A texture layer run that starts at a hardcoded index is one appended texture away from silent breakage.** `TextureLayer::SpawnEggFirst = 38` is correct only while the block texture list has exactly 38 entries, and adding a block would slide all thirty-six egg sprites by one with nothing to notice. The list and the constant are now compared at startup and the mismatch is logged by name. **Any "this enum value equals that container's size" relationship needs a runtime check, because the compiler cannot see it.**
- **Warn about faults, never about a state the user chose.** Three startup lines announced that placeholder reference art was in use \u2014 accurate, and pure noise, because the arrangement is deliberate, documented in three places and visible on screen. The user's verdict: _"remove the warnings, they're excess and just stupid... don't do unecessary things like that."_ What stayed is the diagnostics that catch a genuine fault: a sheet whose dimensions disagree with the code, a missing sprite, a layer constant that has drifted. **The test is whether the line tells the reader something they could not have known and would want to act on.**
- **A guard written as "is this the common case?" hides whatever the uncommon case does.** `ItemEntities::buildMesh` opened with `if (!isBlockItem(drop.item)) continue;` \u2014 so a dropped pickaxe fell, bobbed, could be picked up, and was **invisible**, and had been since tools existed. That was fixed at M20d by routing non-blocks down the *plant* path, which then rendered a sword as **two crossed swords** at 90\u00b0 to each other. Both bugs are the same shape: a branch that answers "what is the common thing" and quietly mishandles everything else. A sprite with thickness \u2014 two quads a few centimetres apart along the spin axis \u2014 is the answer the crossed pair was standing in for, and the comment justifying the cross (*"a single quad turns edge-on and disappears"*) describes exactly the problem thickness solves better.
- **`TextureLayer::SpawnEggFirst` has now moved three times in two sessions, 38 \u2192 54 \u2192 67, and nothing about that is safe.** It is a hardcoded index that must equal the length of a list in a different file; adding one block texture slides all thirty-six egg sprites and, since M20e, the eleven resource sprites behind them. It fails silently and mistextures the lot. The startup check comparing it against the loaded list is what makes this survivable, and it is the general answer: **any "this constant equals that container's size" relationship needs a runtime check, because the compiler cannot see it.**
- **A thickness given as an absolute distance stops meaning anything when you cannot see the object it belongs to.** A dropped sprite was extruded by 0.045 m, which is nearly half the 0.20 m sprite's own width - two quads that far apart read as two parallel swords, and the earlier "crossed swords" fix had only changed the angle between them. The reference extrudes an item by **one texel of its own grid**, and deriving it that way (`kHalfSize / 16`) makes it correct at any size the drop is ever rendered. **When a visual constant is a distance, ask what fraction of the thing it is.**
- **A simplification that names its own blocker will sit there until someone reads the comment as a bug report.** `blockTextureLayer` carried "the mesher cannot say which way a side face points, so the mouth appears on all four" for the furnace *and* the crafting table. It was accurate, and it meant a furnace was a cube with four faces on it. The blocker turned out to be one column: the mesher's face table already listed all six directions and simply threw the horizontal ones away as `BlockFace::Side`. **A `TextureLayer` that exists and is never returned is the tell** - `FurnaceSide` had been loaded into the texture array the whole time.
- **When one id becomes eight, every predicate that named the id has to become a predicate about the family.** Giving the furnace a facing turned two ids into eight, and the dangerous one was not the texture lookup - it was `blockLightEmission`, which tested `id == FurnaceLit` and would have left three of the four directions glowing for nobody. `isFurnace` was already a helper and every user of it came along for free; the ones that had spelled out `case BlockId::Furnace: case BlockId::FurnaceLit:` (blast resistance) were the ones that silently stopped matching. **Grep for the enumerator, not just the helper.**
- **A constant equal to another constant's value, written twice, is the bug shape even when both are right.** `TextureLayer::SpawnEggFirst = 67` and `kResourceSpritesFirst = 67 + kSpawnEggLayers` both carried the literal, so moving one would silently mistexture eleven items while the startup check on the *other* stayed happy. Derived now. **When adding a run of texture layers, append it after every existing run** - the water animation's thirty-two frames went on the very end precisely so no constant had to move.
- **Fog has to fade alpha as well as colour, and the clear colour has to move with it.** A distant translucent surface otherwise stays see-through in the middle of solid fog, and the sky shows through wherever no geometry was drawn - from sixty metres underwater, which is absurd. Both are one line each and both are invisible until you look in the right direction.
- **Our reference textures are Java's and our model geometry is Bedrock's, and for most mobs that difference is invisible.** The two sheets agree for nearly every species, so transcribing a `geo.json`'s UVs works — right up until it does not. The dolphin's tail sits at `(0,33)` in Bedrock and at `(0,19)` on the Java sheet, so its whole back half sampled empty pixels and simply **did not exist**, with no error and nothing in the log. The turtle's head net starts at `u=3` where its geometry says `2`. **After transcribing any UV, dump the alpha of that rect and check coverage** — 100% means the origin is right, partial means it has slid, and empty means the part is about to vanish. `tools/analyze-alpha.ps1` answers it in one command, and it is far cheaper than a render.
- **A `geo.json`'s UVs are only valid for the sheet that geometry ships with, and reaching for them before reading our own texture cost two wrong models in one pass.** The magma cube's Bedrock geometry describes a 64×32 sheet; our reference art is Java's 64×64, and the two layouts have nothing in common — Java runs four slabs down a left column at `u=0` and four down a right column at `u=32`, nine rows apart. Transcribing Bedrock's list produced rects that measured **50% covered**, and the reflex fix — pointing every slab at the one row that measured 100% — was worse, because it **deleted the eyes**, which are painted on the front faces of two specific slabs. `tools/alpha-runs.ps1` on our own texture gave the true net in one command and every rect then measured 100%. **Read the net from the texture you are actually going to sample; a geometry file tells you the box sizes, not where they live.**

## World, chunks, meshing and light

- **Threading revealed that "re-mesh this chunk" and "this chunk changed" are different statements.** They were the same function (`markDirty`) while everything was synchronous, and that was harmless because a mesh was rebuilt immediately. Once meshing became asynchronous the distinction became load-bearing: editing a block on a chunk *face* has to invalidate the neighbour's in-flight job too, or the neighbour installs a mesh built from the old border and nothing ever corrects it. But using invalidation for the merely-unmeshed sweep would discard good in-flight work every time the player crossed a chunk boundary. Hence `invalidateMesh` (bumps the revision) versus `queueMesh` (does not). **This bug was found by reading the code, not by playing — it produces a seam that looks like ordinary chunk popping.**
- **The render distance limit was never a performance limit.** Before touching any code, making the radius configurable and simply raising it showed 383 fps at distance 16 where the game had been shipping distance 5. The real ceiling was **memory** (1.66 GB at distance 24), not frame rate. Had greedy meshing gone in first on the assumption that triangles were the problem, the right fix would still have landed but for the wrong reason, and LOD would have looked necessary when it is not. **Measure which resource is actually exhausted before choosing an optimisation.**
- **Greedy meshing did not need a vertex format change, because the sampler already repeated.** A merged quad simply takes texture coordinates of 0→N and tiles. The merged corners are built by *scaling the existing unit-face corners*, never reconstructed, so the backface winding that was verified on screen at M3 cannot be accidentally reversed. It costs about twice the meshing CPU for 6.4× less geometry.
- **Seeding a flood fill with everything already lit is the difference between 0.7 s and 8.5 s.** The first sky-light pass queued every lit cell as a propagation start point: tens of millions of entries, each costing six neighbour lookups through a hash map, all to discover their neighbours were equally bright. Only cells that can actually spread somewhere are worth queueing — for sky light, those with a horizontal neighbour whose light reaches less far down. **A flood fill's cost is set by how many seeds it starts with, not by how much it changes.**
- **Ambient occlusion partly undoes greedy meshing, and that is arithmetic rather than a bug.** A merged quad interpolates its four corners across the whole span, so it only reproduces the faces it replaced when every one of them was uniformly lit. Merging therefore has to be restricted to flat-lit faces, and geometry rose 2.37×. This was predicted before starting and measured afterwards; do not "fix" it by merging unevenly lit faces, which produces lighting that visibly slides across large quads.
- **Do not conclude a visual feature is broken from one object — confirm the thing being tested is actually in frame.** This has happened twice, in one session. Ambient occlusion looked entirely absent around a block resting on grass, which prompted a hunt through the corner-stepping maths; that block was *floating*, so nothing was adjacent to occlude, and the terrain terraces in the same screenshot had contact shadows the whole time. Then water looked missing at a spot that was above sea level. Both times the code was right. **A one-line log of the value at that position settles it faster than reading code**, and for a subtle effect, **exaggerate it to an absurd value first** — one rebuild turns "is this working?" into an unambiguous yes or no.
- **A face normal can be recovered in the fragment shader instead of stored per vertex.** `normalize(cross(dFdx(worldPos), dFdy(worldPos)))` is exact for flat geometry, which every voxel face is, and it avoided adding 12 bytes to every vertex in the world. It works only because chunk meshes are drawn with an identity model matrix, so the vertex position *is* the world position — if chunks ever gain a per-chunk transform, this breaks silently and everything goes flat-lit.
- **Cave geometry cost tracks surface area, not hollow volume.** Halving the tunnel width took the underground from 21% hollow to 12% and reduced triangles by 3%, because narrow winding tunnels have far more surface per unit volume than open caverns. Lowering the noise *frequency* instead — fewer, bigger caves — cut geometry 25% while making the underground *more* open. **When voxel geometry is too expensive, ask what the surface area is doing, not what the volume is doing.**
- **The user's framing of biomes was better than mine.** I filed them as content needing a design conversation; they pointed out that a biome is first a *developer* abstraction — the table that answers "which block goes here and why" — and the player-facing variety is a consequence. That reframing is what split M15b (the machinery, pure engineering) from the roster (the content). **When a feature looks like content, check whether there is a structural half that can ship first.**
- **Two noise fields, not one, is what makes biome selection work.** A single "climate" value can only order regions along a line, so desert, plains and tundra end up as bands. Temperature and humidity as independent fields give a 2D space where each biome is a point and territory falls out of proximity — and the same weights then give height blending for free, which is what stops neighbouring regions meeting at a cliff.
- **Generation purity forced the *better* structure design, not a worse one.** A tree crosses chunk boundaries, and the obvious implementation writes into neighbouring chunks — which breaks the "pure function of `(seed, chunkCoord)`" rule that all the threading rests on. The alternative sounds wasteful and is not: **each chunk rebuilds every structure that could reach it and throws away the blocks landing outside itself.** The same tree gets built up to four times over, independently, and every copy agrees because it is derived from the seed alone. No cross-chunk writes, no ordering dependency, no deferred-block queue to persist, and it costs a few hash evaluations. **Do not "optimise" this into writing across chunk boundaries** — it would reintroduce a generation-order dependency that only misbehaves under specific thread timing.
- **Put the cheap rejection first, and the ordering is not obvious until you count.** Tree placement originally sampled biome and surface height for every candidate cell, then rolled a hash to find that ~84% of them were empty anyway — three noise evaluations to answer a question one hash already settles. Reordering cut generation from 1912 ms to 1275 ms with no change in output. The guard constant is *derived* from the biome table (`maxTreeDensity()`) rather than written down, because a hand-maintained "max" silently breaks the moment someone adds a leafier biome.
- **The triangle count is a free correctness check on anything that touches meshing.** The first cut of that fix pre-multiplied face shade and ambient occlusion into the two light values. That looked tidy and was wrong: where no light reaches, both terms become exactly zero, so every unlit face compares equal, greedy meshing merges the lot, and the underground loses its ambient occlusion entirely. Nothing errored — but geometry fell from 2,829,100 to 1,487,958 triangles, which is a 47% drop that no lighting change should ever cause. Shading now rides in the spare blue channel instead. **Watch the counter after any meshing change; a large move in either direction is a bug until explained.**
- **Two queues sharing one budget is a starvation bug waiting for the right conditions.** Sky light propagated before block light, and both stopped when the frame budget ran out. Sky light is refilled every frame by chunk streaming, so while moving, block light could simply never run — a placed torch that lights nothing, intermittently, depending on whether you were walking. Block light now goes first: its queue is short, so it costs sky light almost nothing, and it can no longer be crowded out. **Ordering two consumers of one budget is a correctness decision, not a performance one.**
- **Shade came from a narrower predicate, not from a new number.** Making leaves attenuate light looked like it needed a per-block absorption value. It did not: sky light already falls for free only until something stops it, so splitting `isSkyTransparent` out of `isLightTransparent` was enough — leaves let light through but break the free fall, and everything below dims normally. **Check whether an existing rule can be narrowed before adding a quantity to tune.**
- **Foliage is the one place where hiding shared faces is wrong.** Culling the faces between two leaf blocks is the standard optimisation and it makes a canopy a hollow shell — every hole shows sky rather than more leaves, which the user spotted immediately. Leaves also need to be **double-sided**, or looking through a hole shows straight past the block instead of the inside of its far face. Both together cost +6.9% geometry, which is worth it. The trap: with shared faces kept, *both* neighbours emit the boundary, leaving coplanar quads at identical depth. Emitting it only from the positive-facing side saved 4% and removes the depth conflict, and costs nothing because the quad is double-sided anyway.
- **Pixel counts prove a structure the way triangle counts prove a mesh.** The corrected table was verified by counting bands rather than by looking: 20 rim pixels (four 5-pixel corner diagonals), 24 border, 64 grid (a 10×10 box minus nine 2×2 cells), 108 body, 28 dark + 12 plank in the cut corners. Every one matched the reference exactly, which settles "is the geometry right?" before any judgement about colour. `tools/dump-pixels.ps1` exists for this — it was built on the third occasion that eyeballing a magnified sheet missed something, after "all lines and stuff" and the circular corners.
- **Derive an outline from where the neighbouring rows stop, not by drawing it.** Both the furnace flame and the charcoal lump are described as a first and last lit column per row, with the rim computed from whether the row above and below cover each pixel. That produces a clean one-pixel rim on every side including the diagonals, and it cannot be drawn thicker on one side by accident — which is what a hand-authored character map kept doing.
- **A shared helper's hardcoded constant is tuned for whoever wrote it first.** `Set-Dithered` nudged each channel by a fixed +/-20 regardless of its `spread` parameter, which suited the Bramble's dense noise and turned a nearly flat pig into vertical colour static. Anything a caller can dial should scale everything, not some of it.
- **Sky light is seeded once per column and must be un-seeded when the column unloads.** `m_litColumns` recorded which columns had been lit and never forgot, so lowering render distance and raising it again brought chunks back that skipped light propagation entirely and rendered black. Any "have I done this yet?" set that guards expensive work needs a matching erase wherever the work is thrown away.
- **Anything that falls under gravity must first ask whether the ground is loaded.** An absent chunk reads as *air*, so creatures standing over unloaded terrain fell out of the world and reappeared buried when it returned. `World::columnResident` exists for this. Dropped items have the same exposure and have not been fixed.
- **A block without a border has no silhouette, and the user caught it before I did.** Every reference block face carries a 1px darker frame: `furnace_top` and `furnace_side` have byte-identical top and bottom border rows, and the body never goes below luminance 92 while the border sits at 59-79. Without it the block dissolves into the terrain behind it. The count is the check — a 16×16 perimeter is exactly `16 × 4 − 4 = 60` pixels.
- **Two blocks that share a position can disagree, and the saved one wins.** A furnace's lit state is in the block id and its contents are in `furnaces.dat`; bumping that file's version orphaned a lit furnace, which stayed burning with nothing behind it. Whenever state is split between a block and a side table, decide what happens when only one of them survives — here, opening it creates a fresh entry and the next tick puts it out, which is good enough and is written down rather than left to be rediscovered.
- **A block that needs to remember things is a block entity, and it does not belong in `World`.** The furnace is the first of these. Its *lit* state stayed in the block id, where water levels and stair facings already live, because the lit face and the light it casts are properties of the block itself. Its *contents* could not: 233 spare ids cannot hold an inventory. They live in a position-keyed map outside `World` entirely, because chunks are loaded and saved on worker threads and there is no reason to thread block-entity data through that — the whole class of ordering bugs that would buy is not worth it for something a player places a handful of. The cost, written down rather than discovered later: they save when the world saves, not when a chunk unloads.
- **A part that looks like a head is not necessarily a head, and the fix is to ask what a quarter turn does to it.** The wolf's 8×6×7 mane stood upright is a broad slab that swallows the head; laid down the same quarter turn as the torso it becomes the tall narrow shoulder ruff that is the difference between a wolf and a dog. The reference rotates it, and the tell was that the head had nowhere to sit. **When a box makes its neighbour look wrong, try rotating the box rather than moving the neighbour.**
- **An interior face cannot be lit from its own cell, and the bug was three milestones old before anyone saw it.** The partial-shape mesher lit every face from `lightOf({x,y,z})` \u2014 the block's *own* cell, which is solid, so light never propagates into it and it reads 0. For a face flush with the cell wall that is harmless because the sampling steps outward first; for an **interior** face \u2014 a stair's step, a slab's top, a fence rail \u2014 it is pure black. It had affected slabs since M17b and fences since M17d, and nobody reported it because a dark stair edge reads as shading. Interior faces now light from the cell they face, falling back to the brightest of six neighbours if that is opaque too. **The check that it changed nothing else: the triangle count must not move**, because this touches vertex colours and no face counts at all.
- **An animated texture needs no re-meshing if the layer is swapped at draw time.** Water is meshed once with `TextureLayer::Water`, and a spare push-constant slot names the layer to redirect and the frame to use, so the fragment shader substitutes it. Rebuilding chunks to animate a surface would have been the obvious approach and would have cost a full re-mesh several times a second.
- **The triangle counter is only an invariant when the world *and* the camera are identical, and neither is guaranteed across runs.** A 65k drop after a meshing change looked damning; two runs of the *same* binary then gave 282k and 157k, because both were sampled with `pending 372` chunks still streaming. The real explanation was better than a regression: the run in between had saved four modified chunks, which was the new fluid rules deleting the fan of water the old ones had left in the saved world. **Check `pending` is zero and the world is unchanged before comparing the number to anything.** And check the player has not *moved* - an idle soak drifted 80 m and rose 36 m, because the saved position was standing in flowing water and the current carried it into a wall it then auto-climbed. Both behaviours are faithful; the measurement was not.
- **Creature physics had no frame-time clamp where the player's has had one all along.** The resolver snaps out of at most one block, so a chunk-loading hitch was enough to drop a body through the floor. Ported the player's `min(dt, 0.05)`.
- **A ratio whose denominator is also still growing reads high from the first frame.** Meshing progress was meshed-over-*loaded* chunks, so two chunks in with one meshed reported "half drawn" - and it could never reach 1 either, because the outer ring is loaded deliberately and never meshed. Counting over the **visible box**, a denominator fixed by the render distance, makes it start at zero, end at one, and mean something in between. **A progress fraction needs a denominator that is known up front.**
- **The measurable check for "did the loading screen finish too early" is the `pending` counter on the first log line.** It read `pending 372` and `pending 498` before the fix and `pending 0` after, which is far better evidence than watching chunks appear. The counters exist for this; read them before reaching for a stopwatch.
- **A strict gate needs a bail-out that cannot mask the thing it is gating.** Waiting for every visible chunk to be meshed can in principle hang forever. The safe release is not a timeout but a *contradiction*: if every queue is empty while the checkpoints still disagree, nothing will ever move them, so waiting is pointless. An ordinary slow load always has work outstanding and can never trip it.

## Physics, collision and movement

- **A fluid needs a fixed point or it drains itself.** Every flowing cell asks "what supplies me?", and without something that answers "nothing does, and I am still here", the recursion bottoms out at empty. Level 0 water is that fixed point. The corollary is that generated oceans must be *sources*, not merely full cells, or the first hole a player digs quietly empties the sea.
- **A half-height block breaks collision in a way that loops rather than fails.** Adding slabs, the overlap test was updated to respect the shape's height but the *resolution* was not — it still snapped the feet to `floor(y) + 1`, the block boundary. So the player landed at 41.0 above a slab whose surface is 40.5, the ground probe found nothing beneath them, and they fell again: a permanent bounce, which is exactly how the user described it. **Overlap and resolution are two separate places that both encode the shape**, and fixing one without the other produces a cycle rather than a clean failure. Only downward motion needed it — a slab's sides and underside are still on integer planes.
- **"Not solid" quietly stopped meaning "air or water", and it deleted the new block type.** The fluid update skipped solid cells and treated everything else as a fluid candidate, so a cell with no water supply was rewritten to air. That was harmless while every block was a full cube or water. Tall grass is neither, so **every placed plant vanished the same frame** — `setBlock` schedules a fluid update around each edit, while generation does not, which is why generated grass survived and placed grass did not. The fix was to test for air-or-water explicitly. **When a predicate is used as a proxy for a closed set of cases, adding a case to that set breaks every user of the proxy silently** — grep for `isSolid`, `isOpaque` and friends after widening what a block can be.
- **Adding one non-cube shape broke five separate systems, one at a time, and every one was found by playing.** Collision resolution snapped to the block boundary; the fluid update deleted plants; the targeting outline drew a full cage; the raycast treated cell entry as a hit, so aiming at a slab's top from a distance placed the next slab *beside* it; and the outline flashed a full-size cage for one frame after a block was broken, because it read the shape from a ray hit resolved *before* that frame's edits. Each was the same root cause wearing a different hat: **a shape derived somewhere other than the shape table**. The fix that finally stopped the bleeding was making `collisionBoxes` the one definition every system reads. Expect this pattern whenever a long-held invariant is widened — the compiler cannot help, because nothing changed type.
- **That same root cause came back twice more, and the second time proves the first fix was incomplete.** The slab bounce was fixed by teaching *downward* collision resolution to read the shape table. Horizontal resolution was left snapping to `floor(x)`, which nobody noticed because every obstacle up to that point filled its cell horizontally. Stairs do not: the upper step starts halfway across. Walking into one from the high side resolved the player back to the **cell** boundary rather than the **step** face — a teleport of most of a block, which is exactly what the user reported. The lesson is not "fix resolution for stairs"; it is that **overlap and resolution must read the same table on every axis, and fixing one axis is not fixing the bug.** `blockingPlaneAlong` now answers "what face stopped me" for all three.
- **The one place that rule legitimately bends is selection versus collision.** Tall grass has to be walked through *and* be breakable, so it collides with nothing and selects as a slim column. Resist splitting any further: two shape tables is the most a block should need, and the second one earned its place by having a real second case rather than an anticipated one.
- **A bob faked over a constant glide is not a hop, and the physics to do it properly was already there.** The rabbit slid forward at a fixed speed with `sin(gait)` added to its height — which the user called highly unrealistic, correctly, because no part of it was a jump. Replacing it with a real launch velocity and letting the existing gravity and collision produce the arc was *less* code and gave airtime, hop length and arc height for free. The one number that has to be derived is the burst speed: all the ground is covered in the air, so it must exceed the species' average by exactly the fraction of the cycle spent landed. The pose then falls out of `velocity.y` rather than a counter — positive is push-off, zero the apex, negative the descent — which is why the legs extend at both ends of the arc and fold at the top without any of that being authored.
- **Minecraft's numbers are per-tick and ours are per-second; the conversion is not a guess.** `RESEARCH.md` §1.2 has the table. Velocity ×20, acceleration ×400, and drag becomes `d^(Δt/0.05)` — exact at 50 ms and stable at any frame rate. **We have three separate gravity constants and they are deliberately different:** the player's 32 m/s² (`Player.hpp`) matches the reference exactly, creatures use 26 (`Creature.cpp`) so they arc more gently, and dropped items use 22 (`ItemEntity.cpp`) so they settle softly. Do not "unify" them. Anything ported from a wiki page needs the conversion applied or it will be 20× or 400× wrong.
- **A physics constant with a stated derivation can still have the derivation wrong, and the check is a value you already trust.** All four of the water vertical terminals were quoted as coming from the reference's `±0.04` impulse and shipped roughly a quarter too fast, because the water-gravity term was dropped from them — so the surface bob threw the player much further out of the water than it should. The tell was free and was sitting in the same file: **the same expression with no impulse has to reproduce `kSinkSpeed`**, which is measured and correct, and it did not. A published measurement then confirmed the sprint figure to within 3%. When a group of constants share one formula, derive the one you are sure of and check it before trusting the rest.
- **The same control doing two jobs at one speed is what made the water bob violent, and it took three attempts because each fix was aimed at the wrong half.** Holding jump is a **climb** while your eyes are under and a **stroke** once they are out, and both were running at the full swim-up speed - so the player arrived at the surface still doing 2.7 m/s and coasted most of a body length clear. A stroke only has to beat the sink. The user's words after the second attempt were _"my highest point is my entire body above the water, my lowest point is i think half my body underwater ... don't half ass it"_, and they were right to be annoyed.
- **Water tuning is worth simulating rather than playtesting, and it is the rare case where automation beats handing over a build.** The vertical motion is a pure function of the constants, so `tools/simulate-swim.ps1` replays it and prints the bob, the eye height and how much of the body is out. It caught a case the eye would have missed for days: **sprint-swimming settled motionless half a metre up**, because sprint-swimming skips water gravity and that rule is about holding depth *with your head under* - at the surface it meant nothing brought the player back down. The tool **reads every constant out of the headers instead of copying them**, because a second copy of a number that already has a home is this project's most repeated bug. It also let the float height be *derived* rather than judged: `kFloatEye` is the value at which floating in deep water and standing in a single block of it come out identical, which is what makes a shallow pool feel like water instead of like a floor.
- **One threshold settles just as surely as none, and the gap between two is what makes an oscillation.** Holding jump at the water's surface decayed into standing on top of the sea with water physics still running - because the swim-up drive was gated on *any part of the body* being wet, which balances with the **feet** at the surface and a body entirely out of the water. Two fixes in one: aim the kick at the **head**, which puts the body where a treading swimmer's actually is, and latch it between a high and a low mark so it cuts out once the eyes are clear and resumes only after they have sunk back. Neither alone is enough - a single head-height threshold creeps to a stop in exactly the same way, just lower down. Keeping the low mark above zero is what stops the head dipping under on every cycle, which would have flickered the fog and eaten breath.
- **A control that is flattened for one case has to be un-flattened for the other, not left broken.** `moveDirection` drops its vertical component so that looking down cannot walk you into the ground - correct for walking, and it left sprint-swimming able only to skim, so a dive was no quicker than paddling. The fix is one scalar (`PlayerInput::lookY`) read by that one case, with the speed split between horizontal and vertical by the pitch so the total does not change with where you point.
- **A model that eases toward a target speed behaves differently from one that only applies drag, and the difference shows up wherever the two meet.** Bobbing at the water's surface lifts the whole body clear for about a sixth of a second, and our airborne branch spent it accelerating toward the *walking* speed — so swimming forward came out as a lurch on every bounce, which is what the user reported as "the bouncing is weird". The reference has no target speed in the air at all. Patched where it shows rather than rewritten, because the target speed is what makes ordinary movement feel right and only water exposes it.
- **A missing physical response reads as the physics being broken, and the honest fix was not the reference's.** Bedrock separates a slime from the player with `pushable`, which we have no equivalent of at all — so a slime simply passed through and re-entered forever. The user asked for the player's body to be a wall with the slime rebounding off it, which is *better* than the reference here rather than a compromise, and it costs one reflection: keep the tangential velocity, send the inward part back out at a restitution, lift it clear of the floor. **It needs no cooldown**, because after it fires the slime is heading away and the test cannot pass again. Two things that were not obvious: it has to fire on **contact rather than on the blow**, since a small slime does no damage in the reference and a wall does not care; and it has to **mark the creature airborne**, because the hop branch zeroes horizontal velocity on anything it still believes is standing, so a ground-level bounce came out as a hop straight up with the recoil silently discarded.
- **Two questions that both sound like "am I there yet" needed two different tests.** *Arrived* is about footing — the vertical gap within the creature's own `stepHeight`, because anything more is something to climb and both the step-up and the jump read `walking`. *Touching* is about bodies overlapping, and it is **asymmetric**, because the player is 1.8 m tall and a slime sailing over their boots is still hitting them. One symmetric `abs(dy) < height` served neither: it froze a zombie at the foot of a ledge *and* let a slime pass overhead untouched.
- **"Which axes failed to move" is not a test for being blocked, and it silently disables anything built on it.** The jump first fired on `!movedX && !movedZ`, which is never true against a wall: one axis is blocked and the other slides along it freely. Worse, a heading near an axis makes one component tiny, and a 3 cm step rarely overlaps anything, so that axis reports success while the creature is pressed flat against a block. **Probe ahead instead** - foot height blocked, step height still blocked, jump height clear - which is three questions about the world rather than an inference from a resolver. The spiders' wall-climb had been sitting on the same broken test since M20b and only worked diagonally.
- **Adding a movement ability means checking the thing that *chooses* headings, not just the thing that executes them.** The steering fan rejected any heading blocked at foot height, so creatures would have routed around every one-block rise and the new jump could never have fired once. A locomotion ability and the planner that feeds it have to agree on what counts as passable, and the planner is the half nobody remembers.

- **Converting a per-tick *drag* is not the same as converting a per-tick rate, and the naive form silently changes the answer.** A chicken's descent is scaled by 0.6 every tick, which settles at 1.95 m/s. Applying 0.6 per *frame* — or even `0.6^(dt/0.05)`, which looks like the right conversion — lands at 2.55 m/s at 120 fps, because `v = (v - g·dt)·k` has fixed point `g·dt·k/(1-k)` and both `dt` and `k` moved. **Solve for the factor that preserves the fixed point instead**: `k = terminal/(terminal + g·dt)`, which reduces to exactly the reference's constant when a frame happens to be a tick. Any drag ported from a per-tick source needs this; a rate (probability, acceleration) does not.
- **"How high it can step" and "how far it should rise" are different numbers, and using the first for the second turns a step into a teleport.** The step-up lifted by the species' whole `stepHeight`, so everything overshot a slab or a stair by the difference and dropped back under gravity, and the long-legged - horse, llama, frog at 1.0, camel at 1.5 - snapped a full block or more instantly, which is what the user saw. The distance to rise is a fact about the block being climbed and the *world* owns it; `highestSurfaceBelow` already answers it and is what landing uses. Same shape as every other bug in this file: **a value derived somewhere other than the table that owns it.**
- **A tool that writes `settings.cfg` and does not restore it produces a bug report about something else entirely, and this is the third time.** A soak set `spawn_underground=1` to test hostiles and left it there; the user launched the debug build, spawned in a flooded cave, and reported *"gravity is broken, it takes ages to fall"*. Nothing was wrong with gravity - they were swimming, at the 0.22 gravity scale and 3 m/s sink cap. The rule was already written down twice from `benchmark.ps1` leaving `frame_cap=0` behind. **Back up and restore in the same command that mutates, not in a later one** - the gap between the two is where the user launches the game. And when a bug report arrives right after a soak, check what the soak left behind *before* reading any code.
- **A physics state with no visual is indistinguishable from a physics bug.** Water had no underwater view at all: from outside it renders, and the instant your eye goes under, nothing changes except how you move. The user could only describe the symptom as gravity, which sent me through the whole collision resolver looking for a fault that was not there. **When a state changes how something behaves, ask what tells the player it is in that state** - and the fix was one full-screen tinted quad reusing the inventory's existing dim, which is the second time a "new rendering feature" turned out to be an arrangement the renderer already had.
- **A physics fudge that "works" is a fudge that nobody researched, and it will feel wrong in a way the player cannot name.** Water shipped as gravity × 0.22 with a 3 m/s sink cap and horizontal speed × 0.55. Every number was invented, none had a source, and the user's report was that swimming *felt like falling slowly* - which is precisely what a capped fall is. **The reference does not cap anything in water: it multiplies velocity by a drag factor every tick and adds a fixed impulse**, and that one structural difference is the whole of the feel. Sinking, swimming, sprint-swimming, the plunge after a dive and being carried by a current then stop being five features and become one expression with a different terminal in each. Fifteen minutes of research replaced four tuning constants with a model. **When a system's constants have no source, that is the bug report.**
- **Quote a drag system by its terminal speed, not by its impulse, and the frame-rate conversion stops being a problem to solve.** `v = (v − g·Δt)·k` moves its own fixed point when both `Δt` and `k` change, which is the trap the chicken's slow fall sprang once and had to be re-solved for. Writing it as `v = v·k + terminal·(1 − k)` with `k = d^(Δt/0.05)` is exact at *any* frame rate by construction, needs no re-solve, and reduces to the reference's own arithmetic when a frame happens to be a tick. Everything in `world/Fluid.hpp` is quoted that way for exactly this reason. **Reach for the form that cannot be wrong before reaching for the correction.**
- **Floating is a behaviour, not buoyancy, and the reference is unusually clear about it.** Nothing pushes a swimming player or mob upward. A player bobs because *they are holding jump*; a cow bobs because Bedrock gives it `minecraft:behavior.float`, an AI goal that swims up while its head is under. `can_sink: false` is a *third* thing - neutral buoyancy, which holds depth and never surfaces, which is why a squid hangs mid-water and never bobs. Conflating the three would have made every animal a cork. **Real buoyancy does exist as `minecraft:buoyant`** - and the entities that carry it are boats, XP orbs and **dropped items**, not the player. That is why something lost in a lake washes up.
- **A wiki figure that no combination of constants reproduces is probably a copy error, and saying so beats tuning to it.** The Transportation page lists sinking at 0.39 m/s; the constants give 0.50 and nothing produces 0.39. The same 0.39 appears on the Swimming page as the *horizontal* speed of swimming upstream. Chasing it would have meant inventing a drag value to hit a number that was never a sink rate. The four figures that *do* reproduce - 1.960, 3.918, 5.305 and 1.39 - agree to within 0.05%, and agreement that tight is the evidence that the model is right. **Check whether a target number is derivable before treating a mismatch as your error.**
- **`Mojang/bedrock-samples` does not contain everything, and knowing what it is missing saves a fruitless search.** It is a *behaviour pack*: mobs, items, recipes, spawn rules. It holds **no fluid data at all** and `player.json` has no water physics - only a collision box, a movement scalar, breathing and exhaustion. So the fluid constants genuinely have to come from the wiki plus the Java implementation, and the honest thing is to mark which is which. It *is* authoritative for `minecraft:breathable`, `minecraft:navigation.*` and `behavior.float`, and those settled the entire mob half in one pass.
- **Fixing how an entity moves through water is not the same job as fixing how the water moves, and shipping the first while calling the section done left the obvious half broken.** `RESEARCH.md` §9.1 documented the fluid *block* and its own note pointed only at the entity gap, so the entity half got built and the block half was never read against the code. It had been wrong since M15c: water above meant "nearly full", nothing checked whether a cell could fall before letting it run sideways, and there was no weight search at all - so a source on a tower fanned into a cone at every level on the way down. **A section that documents a system correctly is not evidence the system matches it.** When a doc has a "where we stand" note, write one for the whole section, not for the part just worked on.
- **The rule that makes a waterfall a column is a negative one, and it is easy to write the wrong one.** The instinct is "falling water spreads only downward", which Bedrock's own block-state docs say - and it is wrong, because then a fall would never pool at the bottom. The real rule is **"a cell that *can* drop does not spread sideways at all"**, applied to every cell. Mid-air cells all have air beneath them so none of them feeds a neighbour; the one that lands has nowhere to go and pools. The falling *flag* is doing a different job entirely: it marks the cell **full**, which is what gives the landing pool its seventh block.
- **"Can I drain downward" and "do I pool and spread" are two questions, and answering both with one predicate dead-ends every stream.** A cell resting on water can do *neither*: it is partway down a column, not the bottom of one. The first cut asked only "is there water or air below", which correctly kept a waterfall one block wide and then **stopped a flow permanently at the first dip it found** - once the hole filled, that direction still scored as a hole, so the weight search kept steering everything into it and nothing spread past. The fix is Bedrock's own split: drain-into needs a cell that can *receive* water (air, or a plant), while spreading sideways needs **solid ground** underfoot - with a source as the one exception, which is how a lake has a surface at all.
- **A predicate written as "is this passable?" silently became "is this a wall?" for anything new.** Water could not flow into a cell holding tall grass or a flower, so on a meadow every spread came out full of holes and looked directional. The reference **washes plants away and carries them off in the flow**, which is both the correct behaviour and the thing that makes water on grassland look like water. `World` has no idea items exist, so it records what it destroyed and the owning loop turns that into drops - the same split the mesh/upload and generation/apply boundaries already use.
- **Applying gravity and then a drag model in the same frame moves the settling speed by metres per second, and it landed in three places at once.** `v = (v - g dt) k + terminal (1 - k)` settles at `terminal - g dt k / (1 - k)`, which at 120 fps is about **5 m/s of unwanted sink** - so a cow that should bob sank like a stone and a dropped item that should float went straight to the bottom, while every constant in the table still read correct. The player's branch happened to be written as an `else` and was fine; creatures and drops both applied gravity first and then "corrected" it. **A drag model must *replace* the acceleration it is standing in for, never follow it.**
- **A spawn test that re-derives the body's shape will drop things into terrain, and once inside nothing gets them out.** `canStandAt` scanned a column of whole cells `ceil(height)` tall - a second copy of what `collisionBoxes` already owns, blind to a slab, a fence, or a body wider than the cell it stands on. It is now one `overlapsSolid` call on the real box. **The same bug shape as slabs breaking collision, arriving in the spawner.**
- **`Mojang/bedrock-samples` has a `resource_pack/fogs/` folder, and it answers "what does underwater look like" exactly.** Every biome ships a `*_fog_setting.json` with the water fog's colour, start, end and the transition that opens your visibility as your eyes adjust. Two things in it would never have been guessed: `render_distance_type: "fixed"` means the 60 is **metres** rather than a fraction of the render distance, and `fog_start: 0` is why even something close up carries a little of the colour. **The ocean colour `#1165b0` and the general default `#44AFF5` are wildly different** - the default is nearly cyan - so "the water fog colour" is not one number and picking the wrong one looks nothing like the screenshots.
- **A "more physical than the reference" idea was built, hated on sight, and reverted - and the reason it failed is worth more than the idea was.** Bedrock decides underwater fog on one bit: is the camera in a water cell. That means one block of water overhead murks the world, which sounds obviously wrong, so it got replaced with two measurements - how far water reached around the eye, and how much of each view ray lay below the surface. Both are defensible in isolation and together they were **worse in the ordinary case**: dipping your head at the shoreline left a band of "disgusting blue" hanging out over the open ocean, because the two factors disagreed about the same pixel. **The crude rule is crude on purpose - it is the only one that stays consistent across a whole screen.** Do not rebuild this unprompted.
- **A faithful feature can be the thing that feels broken, and `transition_fog` was.** The reference opens underwater visibility from a quarter of the fog distance to all of it over thirty seconds as your eyes adjust. Implemented literally it restarts on every dip, so bobbing at a shoreline - which is most of how anyone meets water - means the density is different every single time you go under and never settles. Every reference screenshot the user produced showed the settled state and none showed the murk. **Removed, and the field that fed it (`Player::submergedSeconds`) removed with it**, because a value only one caller ever reads becomes dead weight the moment that caller goes.

- **`beginHead` rebuilds the frame from `renderPosition` alone, so any lift applied outside it is silently discarded.** The axolotl's model is raised by a constant so it does not sink into the seabed; every part respected that except the head, which dropped through the body the moment it was wrapped in a head group. Nothing warned, because the head was still *somewhere* plausible. **Any helper that reconstructs a transform from scratch will throw away whatever the caller had already applied** — check what the frame contained before wrapping something in one.
- **A body's angle comes from where it is *going*, not where it is facing.** The squid looked wrong for a whole round because it stayed bolt upright while swimming horizontally. Deriving the tilt from the velocity vector — `atan2(horizontal, vertical)`, eased — gives upright when still, flat when cruising and inverted when diving, from one line and with no states.
- **A spatial hash key for graph search must be exact, never a hash.** Two cells colliding in an `unordered_map` key silently splice into one node and the route jumps through solid rock, with nothing to catch it. Packing x and z into 21 bits and y into 12 is exact for a million blocks either side of the origin and a world 96 tall — and where it *would* alias is worth writing down rather than leaving to be discovered.

## Creatures: AI, spawning, behaviour

- **"Make it original" was a requirement nobody asked for, and it would have cost weeks.** The vision documents demanded original mechanics, creatures, recipes and progression, and I carried that into `TIMELINE.md` as a hard rule for Phase 5 without ever checking it against what the user wanted. They wanted a Minecraft-like game they *controlled*, not a new genre. **Ambition inherited from a document is not the same as a requirement from the person paying for it** — and this one was written by another AI (see the note at the top of this file), which is exactly the category of instruction that needed questioning rather than enforcing.
- **Guessing coordinates to reach a test location is not testing, it is stalling.** Verifying cave lighting meant rebuilding and flying for minutes per attempt, and three rounds of guessing spawn coordinates got nowhere and irritated the user. `spawn_x`, `spawn_z` and `spawn_underground` now live in `settings.cfg`; the last one *searches* the surrounding area for the most open cave rather than requiring a column known to contain one. **When a test is hard to reach, fix the reaching** — but reach for the smallest thing that works, and check whether one already exists before building tooling. The same trick found a tree: a throwaway startup scan logged the nearest `Leaves` block, which beat four rounds of guessing at where a forest might be.
- **And a third time, in a system that was never audited because it was written after the fix.** Both of the above were fixed inside `Player.cpp`, and `SYSTEM_MEMORY.md` was updated to say `collisionBoxes` is read "by physics". Creature physics, added three milestones later at M20a, quietly grew its own whole-cell `world.isSolid` test and snapped landings to `floor(y) + 1` — so every creature hovered half a block over a slab or a stair and could not step onto one at all. It produced no bounce, because its overlap test and its resolution were wrong in the *same* direction, which is precisely why nothing looked broken. **A claim in the docs that something has one owner is not a mechanism; the mechanism is a shared function.** `world/Collision.hpp` now holds `overlapsSolid` and `highestSurfaceBelow`, and the player and creatures both call it. **When a new subsystem needs an answer an existing one already has, take the function, do not re-derive the answer** — and when fixing an invariant, grep for every other place that asks the same question rather than fixing the site that reported the bug.
- **A regex that deletes a line deletes whatever else is on it.** Removing a temporary `logInfo` from the creature spawner also removed the `return;` that a previous regex had put on the *same line*, so one spawn attempt became eight and the population overshot its cap by two. It compiled, ran, and looked almost right — the only symptom was a number that should have been 14 reading 16. **Two rules: never let a scripted edit put two statements on one line, and when a count is near but not equal to its constant, that is a bug and not rounding.**
- **"I cannot see it" is not evidence a feature is broken.** Half an hour went into hunting for creatures that were spawning correctly the whole time — the giveaway was already on screen, because the per-second triangle count rose by exactly 144 each time one appeared, and triangles are counted while *drawing*. Reading that number would have settled it immediately. **Add the count to the log before searching the world for the thing you are counting.**
- **A gate that is correct can still be a bad design, and the difference is whether anyone would call it a bug.** Restricting the grazing animals to plains and beaches was faithful to the idea and made the desert — where the world happens to spawn you — lifeless by day. Nothing was broken; it simply looked broken, which for a player is the same thing. Deserts got them. **When a rule makes a place empty, ask whether emptiness is the point or an accident of the table.**
- **The camera and the creatures do not share a yaw convention, and nothing warns you.** `Camera::forward()` is `(cos yaw, sin pitch, sin yaw)`; a creature's is `(sin yaw, 0, cos yaw)`. They are 90° apart, so a showcase laid out along the camera's axis appeared entirely off screen and read as "the creatures did not spawn" — the census in the per-second log said otherwise. Check the census before hunting for missing geometry.
- **The three-state switch in `Creature::think` was replaced at M20c (2026-08-04), and the two ideas that carried it are worth keeping in mind before adding any behaviour.** **Control flags** — three bits, Move/Look/Jump — so a chicken walks and watches at once with no special-casing, and **a row claiming nothing always runs**, which is how targeting works rather than a degenerate case. And **target-production split from target-consumption**: one row writes `target`, attack rows read it, which turned retaliation, pack anger and hunting-on-sight into rows that never mention each other. `RESEARCH.md` §8 records Bedrock's answer and §8.8 the migration; step 5 (unify the two spawners) is the only part still open. Do not port the JSON, the component groups, or a filter language — those exist because Mojang ships to third-party authors, and the component-group system carries a documented "removal resets to default" landmine.
- **Spawning animals at chunk generation was the highest value-per-line item on the list, and it is now built.** The reference runs *two* spawn systems: the continuous cycle near the player and a one-off pass at chunk generation that **ignores the mob cap entirely** and is a pure function of `(seed, chunkCoord)`. That is exactly the shape our generation already had, so it cost no threading risk. It is most of why a world now feels inhabited on arrival rather than trickling in behind you. **`m_populated` only ever grows, deliberately** — a chunk gets its herd once per session, and forgetting it on unload would breed animals out of walking back and forth, which is the same shape as the `m_litColumns` bug in reverse.
- **A creature can need the translucent pass, and the slime is the first.** Its shell is gel over an opaque core carrying the face, so `Creatures::buildMesh` now fills two meshes and the shell is added with `addMesh(..., true)`. Putting it in the opaque mesh instead would blend it against whatever was already in the framebuffer rather than against its own core — the exact lesson water taught at M15c, arriving again on an entity. The first attempt dodged the whole problem by drawing only the shell with the face mounted proud of its front, and the user rejected it on sight as blobs glued to a green box. **When a reference model has a layer you cannot render, check whether the renderer already has the pass before designing around the limitation** — this one had, since M15c.
- **A behaviour that already works as a branch is the easiest thing to move, and the hardest to notice you have broken.** Retaliation, the six-second grudge, `huntsBelowLight` and herd alerting all exist today as special cases inside `Creatures::think` and `strike`. The M20c restructure does not *add* any of them; it relocates them into table rows, which means **the acceptance test is that nothing changes** — a wolf must still ignore you until struck, a spider must still hunt only in the dark. Behaviour that is identical before and after is far easier to verify than behaviour that is new, so port the existing cases first and add `LookAtPlayer` only once they are provably unchanged.
- **A chase with no arrival condition reads as *orbiting*, and the symptom is three steps from the cause.** `MeleeAttack` steered at the player every tick for as long as it had a target, and nothing here collides with the player — so a zombie walked straight through, overshot, and had to come all the way about at a limited turn rate. That is a circle, and because reach is a distance the player sat inside it on every pass and took a hit each time. The user's words were *"attack me by walking through me, then walks around me in circles"*. **The reference does not solve this with a turn rate or a reach; it solves it with a destination** — `melee_box_attack` paths to a node *beside* the target and the path ends there. Anything that closes on something has to say where it stops, and **"it stops when it can hit" is not the same distance as "it stops when it arrives"**.
- **`walking` is sticky, and that is the second time it has bitten.** Claiming the movement controller only stops anything else *steering*; the flag survives from whichever behaviour set it last. The Bramble's fuse learned this once and the melee arrival had to learn it again — anything that stops has to say so outright.
- **A behaviour that claims no controller at all is not a degenerate case \u2014 it is the mechanism.** Bedrock's targeting goals take an empty `control_flags`, which reads like an oversight and is the whole design: because they can never conflict, they always get to run, so "decide what to attack" and "attack it" stop competing. That single property is what turns retaliation, pack anger and hunting-on-sight from three branches into three rows that never mention each other. Do not "tidy" a flagless row by giving it `Move`.
- **`canContinue` exists because *why something keeps going* is a looser question than *why it started*.** Our old hunter hysteresis \u2014 engage inside `senseRange`, give up outside `senseRange * 1.4` \u2014 was an `if`/`else if` pair inside a mood block, and it fell out for free as two predicates on one row. Whenever a state machine has an asymmetric entry and exit condition, that asymmetry is the shape a behaviour table already has a slot for.
- **A defaulted field applies silently, and "everything nocturnal burns off" was never true.** `CreatureSpecies::burnsInDay` defaults to `true`, so the Bramble and the Blackbone burned at dawn purely because their rows stopped before reaching it — nobody ever decided that, and the docs then wrote the accident down as a design choice ("the Bramble does, slimes and spiders do not"). **`RESEARCH.md` §13.4 had the exact list the whole time and only the ordinary undead are on it**: zombie, zombie villager, skeleton, stray, bogged. The user caught the creeper by eye. Two lessons: a field whose default is the *common* case will be wrong for whichever row was written without thinking about it, so **grep the table for rows that stop early** whenever a defaulted flag matters; and when a doc sentence explains why something behaves a certain way, check the behaviour was chosen before treating the explanation as evidence.

- **A function that is only defined is untested, and "it compiles" hides it perfectly.** `Explosion.hpp` shipped `explosionExposure`, `explosionImpact` and `explosionDamage` on day one, and the owning loop ran all three — **for the player only**. Nothing ever applied them to creatures, so the first playtest had Brambles levelling terrain around a completely unharmed goat, and the user found it in one sitting. This is the same shape as M11's dead `RenderStats`: the code was right, complete, and never called. **Wire a calculation to its consumer in the same change that writes it**, or put its result somewhere visible — the blast log now prints how many creatures were hit, which is the number that would have caught this without leaving the terminal.
- **The accumulator bug has appeared four times, and the fourth was in code written the same day as the note warning about it.** All four are a `+=` where several sources write one per-frame impulse. **Creature-hits-player:** seven hostiles landing a blow in one frame applied seven times the knockback, launching the player far enough that every other creature passed the 90 m despawn radius — so the whole map emptied at once and the symptom read as "everything despawns at dawn", three steps from the cause. **Player-hits-creature:** `strike` did `target->velocity += ...`, so each blow landed before the last wore off sent the animal further; the user reported it one message after the first fix, as "each time the knockback increases". **Then both directions of the blast throw.** The rule is settled: **damage sums, displacement does not.** Take the strongest, or assign. When adding any new source of knockback, grep for the shape rather than trusting that the lesson was learned — that is what failed the fourth time.
- **A stimulus flag that can only name one attacker must not be set by a third party.** `provokedTimer` means "the player did this", because `CreatureTarget` has only `None` and `Player`. Setting it from a Bramble's blast would have sent every animal caught in one after the *player*, for something a creeper did — a bug that would have read as "the animals have gone mad" and pointed nowhere near explosions. Left deliberately unset, with the reason written at the call site, because it is exactly the sort of omission a later session would "fix".

- **Never use a movement key as a modifier for a debug action.** `Shift+F8` spawned a charged Bramble, and Shift is *sneak* — so the act of spawning one left the player crouched at 1.3 m/s against a creeper doing 2.4, and backing away, which is the whole defence against an exploder, could not work. The user reported it as "it explodes no matter what", which sounds like a fuse bug and was an input one. Compounding it, the spawn was 5 m out against a 14 m sense range, so it closed and detonated in about two seconds. **A debug spawn needs its own key and enough distance to actually observe the thing** — the point of it is watching, not being hit by it.

- **A number that was never checked against the research is a guess wearing a citation.** The Bramble shipped with health 10, follow range 14, swell 2.5 m and cancel 6 m against `RESEARCH.md` §6.3's 20 / 16 / 3 / 7 - and the comment above the row *asserted* that 2.5 and 6 were Bedrock's while 3 and 7 were Java's, which is exactly backwards. Nothing was measured; the numbers were plausible, so they survived. **When a species row cites the research, open the research** - the file is right there, and the user reporting "their behaviours are off" without being able to say why is what a table full of near-misses feels like from the outside.
- **The detection modifier belongs on `canStart` and nowhere else, and the behaviour table already had the shape for it.** §2.5: a sneaking target cuts follow range to ×0.8 with a 2 m floor, and the modifier is **not** applied once the mob already has you - sneaking stops you being noticed, it is not an escape button. That is precisely the `canStart` versus `canContinue` split, so it cost two predicates and no new state. Whenever the reference distinguishes acquiring from keeping, look for an existing asymmetry before adding a flag.

- **A claimed controller stops other behaviours steering; it does not stop the creature.** `Swell` claims Move, and its own comment claimed that "is what makes it stop walking". It is not: `walking` is **sticky**, surviving from whichever behaviour last set it, so a swelling Bramble kept charging at full speed while it inflated. Anything that claims a controller in order to *stop* has to say so - the flag only decides who may write, not what the previous writer left behind.
- **Smooth what is drawn, not what collides.** The obvious fix - ramp the creature up over several frames - is wrong: the horizontal move has already happened, so a part-way body is inside the block it is climbing, which is a brand new family of stuck states. Snapping the box and easing only the *rendered* position costs one float, cannot affect physics at all, and is what every engine with stair-stepping does. The field has to say so at its definition, because the first instinct on finding it is to use it for something real - reach, targeting or separation reading a deliberately wrong position is a bug that would look like lag.
- **A statistic that is only ever *stored* is untested; a value that is only ever *derived* is unverified.** These are the same failure at two scales, and both have now bitten. `RenderStats` at M11 was computed and never displayed. The Bramble's stats at M20d were written and never checked against `RESEARCH.md`. The ore rarities were invented and never checked against anything. **When a number cites a source, open the source** \u2014 the file or the page is right there, and "the user reports the behaviour feels off but cannot say why" is what a table full of near-misses feels like from the outside.
- **A note saying something is "not done, offered" is a debt, and it does not collect itself.** The creature step-up was smoothed on 2026-08-04, and the same commit left a note that the player had the identical instant step and its camera popped on stairs \u2014 not done, because only mobs had been asked for. The user hit it the next day: _"when i climb up stairs i teleport to each step."_ The fix was four lines and reused the creature mechanism exactly. **When a fix leaves an obvious sibling case unfixed, either do it or put it somewhere that gets read** \u2014 a note in a file nobody opens is not a plan.
- **The wiki is not the Bedrock source, and treating it as one made the same number wrong twice in opposite directions.** The Bramble's swell distances shipped at 2.5 m and 6 m, were "corrected" to 3 and 7 against `RESEARCH.md` §6.3 with a note calling the originals *exactly backwards*, and went back to 2.5 and 6 on 2026-08-05. **`Mojang/bedrock-samples` is the shipped behaviour pack**; `creeper.json` states 2.5 and 6 twice over, in `behavior.swell` and again in `target_nearby_sensor`. §6.3 was quoting the wiki, which on this point is Java's. Repo memory has said "bedrock-samples beats the wiki" for two sessions and the correction went the other way anyway, because the *document* felt more authoritative than the file. **When a research doc and the shipped data disagree, fetch the file** — and when you correct a number, say which source you used, because the next session will only see the number.
- **A field with `must_see` set on every hostile in the reference was missing from ours entirely, and it was the whole bug.** Every "the mobs see me through walls" complaint traced to one absent predicate: `nearest_attackable_target` was checking distance and nothing else, and only the Bramble's fuse ever cast a sight ray. **The lesson is where to look, not what the fix was.** A behaviour that is a table of tunable numbers hides missing *predicates* far better than it hides wrong values, because a wrong value is visible in the row and an absent condition is visible nowhere. When behaviour feels wrong and every number checks out, list what the reference *tests* and diff that against what we test.
- **Units are mixed inside a single Bedrock component and reading them uniformly is a 20x error.** `nearest_attackable_target` has `scan_interval` in **ticks** and `attack_interval`, `persist_time`, `must_see_forget_duration` and `entity_types[].cooldown` in **seconds**, all on the same component. Separately, `avoid_mob_type` has **two different `max_dist` fields** on one page \u2014 goal-level default 3, per-entry default 16. And `delayed_attack` has no `cooldown_time` at all (`attack_duration` doubles as it) while silently flipping `track_target` to true. `RESEARCH.md` \u00a71.2 covers the per-tick conversions; these are the traps it does not.
- **Three incompatible melee reach models exist and picking the wrong one is a factor of two and a half.** `melee_attack` multiplies body size by 2, `delayed_attack` by 1.5, and `melee_box_attack` throws that out and expands the mob's own AABB by `horizontal_reach` 0.8. **Every modern Bedrock mob uses the box form**, which is why a flat 1.5 m reach gave our silverfish a polar bear's bite and our polar bear a silverfish's. Reach is a fact about the body, and the body already knows its own width.
- **A soft separation impulse has to respect the world, or a herd buries its own members.** `separate` wrote straight to `position` with no collision test, so creatures crowding a wall shoved each other into it - and a body inside a block finds every move it tries overlapping, so it is stuck permanently. Testing before committing is two lines. **Any code that writes a position directly rather than going through the resolver needs the same check.**
- **Something will end up inside terrain anyway, so there has to be a way out.** A shove, a spawn over ground that had not finished loading, or a player filling in the block a creature stood in all put a body inside a block, and every one of those is permanent without an escape. Climbing out is cheap, runs only when actually overlapping, and covers every entry point at once - spawn eggs, the debug key and the showcase included - which is far better than validating each of them separately.
- **Our spawn shell was 12-30 m against the reference's 24-44.** Close enough to watch one appear out of nothing, which is most of what "they spawn anywhere" meant.
- **Minecraft has no water-body size test, and I invented one before checking.** I wrote a flood-fill to stop fish spawning in puddles. The reference does it with a **biome tag** — `spawns_underwater` plus an ocean/river filter — which costs nothing and is why a village well never fills with cod. What the spawn rules actually carry is `height_filter`, `density_limit`, `herd`, `distance_filter` and `brightness_filter`, and every one of those is a number rather than a search. **Fetch `behavior_pack/spawn_rules/<mob>.json` before designing a spawn condition.**
- **A debug view that freezes the world cannot review anything that moves, and pretending otherwise wastes a round.** `creature_showcase` was used to check a squid's tentacles and a pufferfish's inflation, both of which are animations, and it showed neither. It got one accommodation — an inflating species shows its three *stages* instead of three angles, because two thirds of its model is otherwise unreviewable — and everything else goes through a spawn egg. Two older faults surfaced in the same pass and both were the showcase writing to the real world: it placed its subjects before `loadCreatures()` ran, and it **saved the result on exit**, dropping the whole roster into the player's save.
- **A system that "sort of works" usually has a constant naming exactly how much it can see, and that constant is the bug report.** The steering fan was described as local planning for four milestones; what settled what it could and could not do was `kProbeDistance = 0.9` — **less than one block ahead**, with no memory. Every symptom followed from that one number: a wall longer than the probe gets hugged rather than rounded, a dead end gets oscillated in, and a detour that temporarily increases the distance to the goal can never be chosen, because every candidate is scored on the heading it wants *right now*. **Look for the horizon constant before theorising about the behaviour.**
- **Two independent triggers for the same expensive work will find a state where one of them fires every frame.** A route wants rebuilding when it is finished *and* when the target has moved, and letting either fire on its own meant a chaser dancing in and out of contact cleared its route on the frame it arrived and searched again on the next one, forever. Gating **both** behind one cadence costs nothing, because the fallback in the meantime is the behaviour that existed before. Whenever a second reason to redo something is added, check what happens when it is true continuously.
- **Reconstructing a path walks it backwards, so truncating it truncates the wrong end.** Capping the route at 32 waypoints while walking parents back from the goal keeps the 32 nearest the *goal* — so the reversed route begins somewhere the creature is not, and it sets off in a plausible but completely wrong direction. Count the chain first, then write it out forwards. The general shape: **any cap applied during a reversal is applied to the wrong end.**
- **A default that only matters before the first tick still matters, because the review tool never ticks.** `Creature::onGround` defaulted to `false`, and the frozen showcase therefore drew every magma cube permanently mid-leap with its slabs flung apart — the one pose that is not worth reviewing. Defaulting it to `true` costs nothing, since `step` recomputes it every tick before anything reads it. **When a pose is chosen by simulation state, ask what that state reads as in the showcase.**


## Interface, HUD and inventory

- **Do not claim a bug is fixed when the cause was never actually found.** During the same session a fix was shipped for the "first click after switching block types does nothing" report with an honest admission that the root cause could not be located by reading the code, only that the suspect mechanism (a press queue) had been removed. That framing was correct and should be the default: state what was changed, state what remains unverified, and ask for a re-test. Claiming a confident diagnosis that later proves wrong costs far more trust than admitting uncertainty up front.
- **A HUD element drawn in the wrong depth order is invisible, and nothing reports it.** The hotbar's selection highlight was drawn correctly for two iterations and could not be seen, because the bar's frame sat nearer the camera and covered it completely. There is no error, no warning, and the geometry is genuinely being submitted. **When a HUD element does not appear, check its depth against everything drawn near it before touching its geometry.** The general fix that worked: give the selected cell an entire depth band nearer than every other cell, rather than trying to interleave one quad among them.
- **Numbers without labels are not a UI, and avoiding a font atlas was a false economy.** The first M11 overlay drew seven-segment digits in nine colour-swatched rows and logged the colour key to the console. The user's verdict was blunt: _"what is that.. no labels, nothing, font also looks like a joke. color scheme and detailing looks like a joke."_ They were right — a reader cannot hold a nine-colour key in their head, and hand-drawn segments render zeros as broken `C` shapes. The replacement is a hinted 1-bit Consolas atlas generated by `tools/make-font.ps1`, bound as a third texture, and it was less code than the segment renderer it deleted. **When a diagnostic needs words, build the font.**
- **That guard is not airtight, and the failure is silent and mildly dangerous.** A later run captured the VS Code window instead of the game and sent it a one-second left-button hold; nothing was damaged, but the measurement read `0` and looked like a broken fix rather than a broken test. **Check the screenshot before trusting the number it came with** — the image said "this is the editor" immediately, while the count said nothing. When a click-driven test disagrees with a code reading, suspect the harness first.
- **UI artwork should be shipped as a texture, not rebuilt out of primitives.** The first inventory screen was hand-drawn from quads and looked nothing like the concept. The artist's image is now composited into the HUD sheet by `tools/make-hud-sheet.ps1` and drawn as one sprite, with item icons on top — which is how the original does it too. Two details make it work: the art arrives as an **integer upscale**, so sampling every Nth pixel recovers the real grid exactly where resizing would blur it; and every slot position is **measured off the art in its own pixels** and scaled through one constant, so a revised mock-up needs only those numbers changed.
- **A guard meant to skip pointless work made an item impossible to retrieve.** Creative mode skipped item pickup entirely, on the reasoning that a creative inventory has no need of what it collects. The consequence was that anything dropped in creative could never be picked up — and because the attraction code pulls a drop toward the player and then falls through to gravity once it arrives, the item visibly bounced off the player forever. Two bugs stacked: **the skip should have been "do not spawn drops", not "do not collect them"**, and the attract branch needed no lower distance bound, because falling through to physics *at the moment of arrival* is what produced the oscillation. When skipping work for a mode, check what the skipped work was holding up.
- **A UI action that commits only on release gives the player nothing to judge by.** The first click-drag distributed a stack across slots when the button came *up*, which is correct and felt broken: nothing moved during the sweep, so there was no way to tell whether it was working. The fix is not to add a preview alongside the real thing — it is to **apply the real thing every frame and replay it from scratch as the drag grows.** That needs each dragged slot's contents from *before* the drag, so the distribution can be rewound and recomputed rather than applied incrementally, which would compound. Rewinding also makes "a sweep that never left its first slot" trivially an ordinary click.
- **Do not arm a drag from the click that filled the cursor.** Picking a stack up leaves it on the cursor with the button still down, so the obvious implementation starts a sweep immediately — and then moving away from the slot you just took from scatters the stack back out. A sweep may only begin from a cursor that was **already** carrying something when the button went down.
- **Measure distance from the corner, not from the centre, or a cut corner becomes a curve.** The first crafting table top used a centred Manhattan radius (`|x-7.5| + |y-7.5| <= 10.5`) and then painted a rim over every boundary pixel. That draws a *diamond* whose traced, randomised outline reads as a circle — the user's reaction was "why are the textures curved and circular, the whole point of minecraft is to be blocky." The reference is simply `min(x+y, ...) < 4` from the nearest corner, which produces a 45° step with nothing to trace. **The general trap: any silhouette built by testing a boundary and then decorating it will round itself off.** Build the shape so the steps fall out of the arithmetic instead.
- **Derive a new UI panel from the existing one rather than drawing a second.** The crafting table's panel is generated from the inventory's by `tools/make-hud-sheet.ps1`: frame, backdrop, storage rows and hotbar copied verbatim, top section cleared, and the *same 18×18 cell* stamped into new positions. The cells are literally the same pixels, so the two screens cannot drift apart, and revising the artwork updates both. The layout numbers came from measuring the reference GUI, which shared our rows exactly and differed by one pixel in its columns — worth checking before inventing coordinates.
- **A shared sheet size is a drift hazard the compiler cannot see.** `kSheetSize` was duplicated in `Hotbar.cpp` and `InventoryScreen.cpp`; adding the crafting panel grew the image and would have skewed every HUD sprite in whichever file was forgotten. It now lives once in `HudPrimitives.hpp`. **When an asset's dimensions appear in code, they belong in exactly one place.**
- **The user is a tool; ask them before building test scaffolding.** Verifying the crafting screen looked like it needed a temporary code hook to force the panel open. The user interrupted with _"or you could have me verify it? i'm a tool remember"_ — which is exactly the working agreement already written at the top of this file. Automating a playtest is worth it for something repeatable or hard to reach; for "does this screen render", hand over a build and ask. **Said again on 2026-08-01** after a click-driven test misfired into the editor: _"remember i can be a testing tool when i'm here."_ The rule now has a sharper edge — **anything needing aim, a held button, or a judgement about feel goes to the user while they are present**, because the harness cannot aim and has twice sent input to the wrong window. **Said a third time on 2026-08-02, in capitals** — _"REMEMBER I'M A TOOLLLLL"_ — after a showcase capture burned four commands on a window that had opened the inventory by itself and then vanished. Two rounds of a screenshot harness not working is the signal to stop, not to try a fifth time: **restore the settings and the save, hand over the build, and say what to look at.** Note the flip side, though: the earlier report that a block icon was missing turned out to be **their own play session** having moved the item, so reproduce from a clean state before diagnosing.
- **`Add-Type` cannot redefine a type, so a helper class in a script has to be versioned.** Editing an inline C# block fails forever in an already-running shell: adding mouse-click support to `capture-window.ps1` meant renaming `WinCap3` to `WinCap4`, or any shell that had already loaded the old definition would silently keep using it and the new method would appear to be missing. Guard with a `PSTypeName` check and version the class name. The same trap applies to every `Add-Type` block in `tools/`.
- **A gap you can justify from a measurement is not a random gap.** The catalogue tabs are 22 units wide on a 25-unit pitch, so the 3-unit space between them falls out of the reference's own numbers rather than being chosen; Search is then pushed a further 21 units right and lands 3 short of the card edge, which is exactly the card's frame width. Two of those numbers were originally guessed (I had Search flush right at 124 against the measured 121). **When a layout constant can be derived from the measurement, derive it and say so in the comment** — otherwise nobody can tell later which numbers were read and which were invented.
- **A HUD that only rebuilds on demand must be told when a thing stops, not just while it happens.** The dig progress bar was drawn whenever `breakProgress > 0`, and the rebuild condition used the same test — so the frame progress returned to zero, nothing asked for a rebuild and **the last bar drawn stayed on screen forever**. It looked like digging was stuck. Any transient HUD element needs its disappearance to dirty the HUD as much as its appearance does.
- **Automated playtesting cannot aim.** `SetCursorPos` does nothing while the game holds the cursor, because the camera reads *raw* input and raw input only sees real movement — `mouse_event(MOUSEEVENTF_MOVE, …)` is what works. Before that was understood, a dig test aimed past the 12 m reach at nothing and looked like a broken mining system; the giveaway was **no selection outline in the screenshot**. Check for the outline before concluding anything about breaking or placing.
- **An instant action inside a per-frame loop repeats at the frame rate, and the symptom looked like a spell being cast.** Timed digging (M19c) short-circuits creative mode with `seconds = 0`, which makes `breakProgress` reach 1.0 *every frame* the button is held. Each break exposes the block behind it, so one 60 ms click stripped twelve blocks in a straight line out to the full reach - the user described it as "it throws lightning in that direction and decimates all blocks in click range". Survival was never affected, because a timed break is paced by its own progress bar, which is exactly why it survived every test. **The fix is a cooldown on the instant path only**; putting one on both would make ordinary mining stutter. Whenever a duration can legitimately be zero, ask what happens when the work it guards is free.
- **A held-button feature needs testing at both ends of its range.** This bug required only holding left click in creative, which is the single most common thing a player does, and it shipped anyway because verification was automated screenshots rather than play. The user found it in seconds. **Anything driven by a held button should be exercised held, not clicked once.**
- **An icon draws two side faces at once, and they do not point the same way.** `appendBlockIcon` passed `BlockFace::Side` with no direction for both the front and the right quad, so a furnace showed its mouth on both — in the hotbar, the inventory and the catalogue. The world mesher had been fixed for this a day earlier and the icon was never looked at, because "the furnace faces the right way now" was true of the only place anyone had checked. **A defaulted parameter that means "I have nothing to tell you" will be passed by every caller that has something to tell you and did not think about it.** `blockFacing` and `quarterTurn` answer `Unknown` for the great majority of blocks, so every other icon is byte-identical — which is the property that made the change safe to make at all.
- **A dropped non-block item rendered nothing at all, and had done since tools existed.** `ItemEntities::buildMesh` opened with `if (!isBlockItem(drop.item)) continue;` — so a pickaxe thrown on the floor fell, bobbed, could be walked over and picked up, and was **invisible the whole time**. Nothing reported it because everything else about the drop worked. It only surfaced when spawn eggs made non-block drops common. The fix was already sitting in the same function: the crossed-quad path that plants use, which exists precisely because a sprite has no cube to wrap. **When a code path is guarded by "is this the common case", check what the uncommon case actually does rather than assuming it is handled elsewhere.**
- **An icon drawn from a shape table still has to read the shape table.** `appendBlockIcon` drew one isometric box at `shapeHeight`, so stairs showed as a plain cobblestone cube in the hotbar, the inventory and as a drop. Slabs were already right, which is *why* nobody looked: half a cube is one box, and a stair is two. Same root cause as every other bug in this file \u2014 a shape derived somewhere other than the table that owns it \u2014 arriving in the one place nobody thinks of as geometry.
- **A catalogue entry is a source, not a container slot, and that one fact settled every question about it.** Measured from `Mojang/bedrock-samples`: the right card's slots are `container_slot_button_prototype`, while catalogue cells are `creative_no_coalesce_container_slot_button` with actions `recipe_select`/`_secondary`/`_tertiary`. It follows without further research that an entry **never depletes, can never be placed into, and has no double-click gather path** \u2014 three behaviours we would otherwise have had to decide. **The empty space around the entries is the bin**, which is why Bedrock needs no trash slot. Worth reading the UI data before designing a UI; it answers questions you did not know to ask.
- **A hit test that succeeds and then does nothing swallows the click.** A catalogue cell past the end of the item list still hit-tests as a cell, so the first implementation returned "yes, a cell" and then `continue`d \u2014 and the empty lower half of a short tab silently ate every click instead of falling through to the bin. **When two regions overlap and the inner one is sometimes inert, "did I hit it" and "did I consume it" are different answers** and the code needs both.
- **Units are mixed inside a single shipped component, and `inhale_time` is the trap.** `minecraft:breathable` gives `total_supply: 15` in **seconds** while the internal counter is 300 **ticks**, and `suffocate_time` is documented as seconds while its default of −20 is plainly the tick threshold. Worse, **the wiki and the shipped data disagree on the refill rate** - the wiki says one bubble every 4 ticks (40 total), `inhale_time: 3.75` says 75 ticks. The wiki's figure looks like "+4 air units per tick" read wrong. Shipped data wins, and the disagreement is written down rather than silently resolved.
- **Widening a run of item ids moves every id behind it, and item ids are in the player's save.** Adding ten spawn eggs to the existing run would have shifted Coal, the ingots and the buckets, silently turning a saved inventory into different items. The answer was a **second run at the very end** of both the item enum and the sprite layers, with `allItems()` re-ordering the two so the catalogue still shows one unbroken block. **Anything persisted by number can only ever be appended to.**

## Saving, settings and persistence

- **Amdahl's law arrived immediately and should have been predicted.** Eleven workers cut generation and meshing by 3.3×, and total startup by only 1.6×, because uploading meshes to the GPU is serial main-thread work that threading cannot touch. The fix was not to tune the threading further but to **measure the two phases separately** so the remaining serial cost was visible instead of being blamed on poor scaling. Split a timer before optimising against it.
- **A screenshot tool that repositions the window will also save that state.** Forcing the camera to look east for a sun screenshot meant the game saved that orientation to `player.dat` on exit, so the next normal run started staring at the sky. Harmless here, but any test hook that mutates persisted state needs to either restore it or run against a throwaway save.
- **A diagnostic that reports the configuration instead of the state will send you the wrong way.** Passives appeared not to spawn, so a line was added logging the biome at `spawn_x`/`spawn_z` — which said `Mountains` while the player stood in a desert, because **a saved player position overrides the spawn setting entirely** and the log never knew. Two rounds of "why is the biome table broken" came out of trusting it. The line now reports the biome under the player's *actual* position. **Log the value the code will use, not the value that was requested.** (The save is at `saves/world_<seed>/player.dat`, not `saves/player.dat` — deleting the wrong path fails silently and looks like the setting being ignored again.)
- **Do not link a build while the game is running.** The release link failed with `LNK1104: cannot open file 'bin\game.exe'` because the process still held it during shutdown. Worse, the tempting fix is to force-kill the game, and **force-killing skips the save path entirely** — `world.saveAll()` and the furnace write both run after the window closes. Send `Alt+F4` and wait for `Shutting down.` in the log instead.
- **A loading screen that streams around the wrong point finishes honestly and still leaves you waiting.** The world was loaded around `spawn_x`/`spawn_z` and the player was then placed at the *saved* position, which in a real save is thousands of blocks away - so the bar measured a piece of world nobody was about to stand in, the game started, and the real area streamed in underneath the player. Every checkpoint was telling the truth about the wrong thing. **When a progress bar disagrees with what you can see, check what it is measuring before changing how it is measured.** The fix was to read the saved position *before* the loading screen, since it is what the world has to be centred on; only the fresh-world placement has to wait for chunks, because it reads blocks.

## Build, tooling and PowerShell

- **A plain shell cannot build this project, and the failure looks like a broken install.** CMake's Ninja generator locates MSVC through `PATH`/`INCLUDE`/`LIB`, which only exist inside a Developer environment. Verified directly by configuring from a scrubbed shell: `No CMAKE_CXX_COMPILER could be found`. An earlier version of `SYSTEM_MEMORY.md` confidently claimed CMake would find MSVC on its own via the Visual Studio registry — that is true only for the `Visual Studio` generators, not for Ninja. **The claim was written from plausible reasoning and was simply wrong; it was caught only because it was actually tested before being trusted.** `tools/dev-env.ps1` now exists so this is one command instead of a rediscovery.
- **VS Code's CMake Tools needs `architecture`/`toolset` with `"strategy": "external"` in the preset**, or it cannot build a Ninja + MSVC project either — same root cause as above. Those fields are ignored by CMake itself for Ninja, so they are free on the command line and load-bearing in the editor. Separately, `Cannot configure: No configure preset is active` just means no preset has been selected yet (`Ctrl+Shift+P` → `CMake: Select Configure Preset`); it is not an error in the preset file.
- **The VS Code integrated terminal mangles multi-line PowerShell input.** Observed 2026-07-30: a multi-line script with a `function` definition and blank lines came back as the single garbage token `inue'` — the tail of `SilentlyContinue'`, meaning the input was fragmented mid-paste. Nothing was wrong with the script itself. **Always send one line, chaining with `;`.** For anything genuinely long, write a `.ps1` file and invoke it, or base64-encode it and use `powershell -EncodedCommand`.
- **`Start-Process -UseNewEnvironment` is broken in PowerShell 5.1** — it strips variables PowerShell itself needs and dies with `Internal Windows PowerShell error ... 8009001d`. To test something in a clean environment, launch a child shell and reset `Path` from the Machine/User registry values by hand instead.
- **`cl.exe` not being on `PATH` does not mean MSVC is missing.** MSVC deliberately only appears on `PATH` inside a "Developer Command Prompt." To check whether MSVC exists at all, look for `vswhere.exe` at `%ProgramFiles(x86)%\Microsoft Visual Studio\Installer\vswhere.exe` instead. (On this machine it genuinely was missing — but the check matters, because the naive `Get-Command cl` test reports "missing" even on a correctly configured machine.)
- **Recover a reference image's native pixel grid by measurement, not by eye.** The HUD art arrived as a 1480×328 WebP. Probing runs of identical pixels along one row showed every run was a multiple of 8, proving an exact 8× upscale and giving a lossless 185×41 original by nearest-neighbour downscale. Guessing the scale would have produced half-pixel seams that look like anti-aliasing bugs forever after. `tools/probe-image.ps1` exists for this; `tools/convert-image.ps1` handles WebP, which neither stb_image nor System.Drawing can read.
- **Widening `Vertex::color` from vec3 to vec4 silently made the crosshair invisible.** `engine::Vertex{{...}, {c.r, c.g, c.b}, ...}` still compiles against a `float[4]` — aggregate initialisation just zero-fills the missing element, which is alpha, which with blending enabled means fully transparent. No warning at `/W4`. **After changing a vertex field's width, grep every brace-initialised `Vertex` rather than trusting the compiler.**
- **`SendKeys` goes to whatever currently has focus, and `SetForegroundWindow` usually fails from a background process.** An unverified send while automating a screenshot typed `F5` into VS Code instead of the game — which is the *start debugging* shortcut. `tools/capture-window.ps1` now compares `GetForegroundWindow()` against the target handle and refuses to send or capture otherwise. It also waits ~1.2 s after focusing, because the game clears buffered input on focus change and swallows a key sent immediately.
- **A benchmark that writes the settings file the game is played from will be mistaken for a bug — twice.** The M13b sweep set `frame_cap=0` and left it there, so the user launched the game and reported the frame limiter broken. It was fixed, then broken again by the very next sweep. The limiter was fine both times. `tools/benchmark.ps1` now backs the file up and restores it in a `finally` block. **Any tool that mutates state the user also uses must restore it, not merely intend to.**
- **A `Get-Process` main window handle can be the console, not the game.** `tools/capture-window.ps1` captured a black console window because the run was launched *without* output redirection, so `game.exe` owned a console whose handle won. It also needs `AttachThreadInput` to reliably take the foreground: `SetForegroundWindow` alone silently fails from a background process, and since the capture reads screen pixels, a window that is not on top captures whatever is. `GetClientRect` plus `ClientToScreen` is the tidier way to find the region, and it silently leaves the origin at (0,0) when the conversion fails, capturing the top-left of the desktop instead — `GetWindowRect` gives absolute coordinates with nothing to go wrong.
- **`â€”` in terminal output is not a corrupt file; it is the console.** An onboarding review reported mojibake in `Biome.hpp`, and the grep output did show `â€”`. The file was fine — PowerShell's console renders a legitimate UTF-8 em-dash through cp1252 and produces exactly that sequence. **The test that actually answers the question is counting `U+FFFD` replacement characters** (`$text.ToCharArray() | Where-Object { $_ -eq [char]0xFFFD }`); zero means the bytes are valid UTF-8 no matter what the terminal drew. Eleven other source files "fail" a naive non-ASCII grep for the same harmless reason. Real corruption *did* happen once in this project — `Set-Content` defaults to ANSI and silently mangled nine em-dashes in the docs — so the concern is legitimate, but the two are distinguished by the U+FFFD count, never by eye.
- **⛔ The U+FFFD test does NOT catch double-encoding, which is the corruption `Get-Content` actually causes.** Bare `Get-Content` in PowerShell 5.1 reads a UTF-8 file *with no BOM* as Windows-1252, so every multi-byte character becomes two or three separate chars; writing it back as UTF-8 then encodes each of those again. The result is `â€”` **stored in the file** rather than merely displayed — and it is perfectly valid UTF-8, so **`U+FFFD` counts zero and the file passes the project's own check.** This corrupted four documents during the 2026-08-06 cleanup and was caught only because a later `replace_string_in_file` could no longer match an em-dash it had just written.
  - **Read with `Get-Content -Encoding UTF8`** (or `[System.IO.File]::ReadAllText`, which honours the BOM), and write with `[System.IO.File]::WriteAllLines(path, lines, (New-Object System.Text.UTF8Encoding($false)))`.
  - **The reliable detector is the character `Ã` (U+00C3).** It is the first half of every double-encoded sequence and appears in essentially no honest English prose. Count it alongside `U+FFFD`, never instead of it — they catch different failures.
  - **Back up before any scripted rewrite of a document.** The only reason this cost minutes rather than a day is that every file had been copied to `docs-backup/` first.
- **PowerShell variable names are case insensitive, and it bit again in that very tool.** A `-FloatEye` parameter and the `$floatEye` read out of the header are **the same variable**, so the override silently did nothing and a sweep printed four identical rows. Same fault as the `$B`/`$b` colour-mixing bug. Any script parameter that shadows a local needs a distinct name, not a distinct case.

## Process and working method

- **Do not infer world orientation from a rotating object's face colours.** While diagnosing the above, the cube was spinning on an arbitrary axis, so face colours (painted in model space) said nothing about which way was up. A confident claim that "left and right faces are both visible, culling is broken" was made from exactly this reasoning and was simply false — both screenshots showed three mutually adjacent faces, entirely valid. **The user proposed the fix: freeze the object in a known orientation, put it on a ground plane, and move the camera instead.** That turns "the camera is above, so I must see the bright top face" into a falsifiable test. Adopt this shape of experiment for any future "is the geometry right?" question.
- **A wide field of view close up looks like a bug and is not.** At 60° with the camera near the subject, perspective foreshortening made a cube look like a warped trapezoid. The projection maths was verified correct (horizontal and vertical scale factors produce identical on-screen size for a square). Fixed by dropping to 45° and moving the camera back. Check the maths before changing it, but treat "looks wrong" as worth investigating either way.
- **Say what success is supposed to look like before showing it.** The user reasonably asked whether "it just shows a shade of blue, that's it" was a problem — it is precisely the milestone's goal, but that had not been stated plainly enough up front. When a deliberately minimal result could be mistaken for a broken one, name it in advance.
- **A stuck input looks exactly like a physics bug, and the reported symptom was three layers away from the cause.** On 2026-07-30 the user reported that crouching at a block edge sometimes let them fall off, but only after holding Shift + Ctrl and breaking blocks for a while. Two theories were pursued and both were wrong: first that auto step-up bypassed the sneak edge guard (a real hole, fixed, but not this bug), then that some subtle ordering in the guard was at fault. The actual cause was that `glfwGetMouseButton` had latched to "pressed" — the user's trackpad double-tap generated a press whose release was never delivered — so the game was silently mining whatever the crosshair touched, **including the ground under the player's own feet**. The edge guard was working perfectly; there was genuinely nothing left to stand on. **Two lessons.** Polled hardware state you cannot reset is a liability: track button state from press/release events in our own `Window` and clear it on focus loss, so there is always a way out. And when a physics symptom only appears in combination with an *input* action, suspect the input before rewriting the physics — the tell was that the second report ("blocks break wherever I look, whether or not I'm touching the trackpad") was the *same bug* stated plainly, and it arrived only after two failed fixes to unrelated code.
- **A single timed run is not a measurement.** Startup timings varied by 40% run to run, which produced a table where one worker looked slower than zero and eight looked slower than four. Three runs reporting the minimum made the picture monotonic and obvious. Always repeat before concluding anything from a wall-clock figure.
- **Verify culling by the fraction of geometry drawn, not by looking for holes.** Frustum culling that is slightly too aggressive removes geometry only at the screen edge, exactly where it is hardest to notice in a screenshot. Comparing triangles drawn against the total gave 29.4% versus the ~28% a 100° horizontal field of view should cover — a single number that would have moved obviously if the planes were wrong.
- **The hardcoded `surface <= kSeaLevel ? Sand : Grass` was spotted by the user, not by me.** It had sat there since M5, producing a dead-flat contour line across the entire world, and it meant most block types could never occur naturally because height was the only input. It was a reasonable first pass that had stopped being reasonable several milestones earlier, and nothing flagged it. **A placeholder with no owner becomes permanent** — when one goes in, name the milestone meant to replace it.
- **Creative mode means "placing is free", nothing more.** It has now caused two bugs by meaning more than that: suppressing pickup left dropped items orbiting the player forever, and suppressing drops meant breaking a block in creative destroyed it. The second was the user's call to fix (2026-07-31): _"items should drop no matter what gamemode i'm in."_ **Resist adding mode checks to anything except the cost of placing** — every one is a branch where the two modes can behave differently and only one gets tested.
- **Whether it is one thing or three is a question about the art, not the code.** The furnace's burn indicator is *three* flame licks in the reference, and the user's own mockup shows three grey strokes — so the first attempt drew three, and at 4 pixels wide each they read as lit matches. The user had said "**the** flame is orange bordered and yellow in the middle/core", which describes a rim and a core, and a rim and a core need width. One flame across the full 14 pixels worked immediately. **Read the user's description as a spec and the reference as context, not the other way round.**
- **Every structural image metric here is computed on luminance, so none of them see colour.** A muted pink and a vivid coral can match face for face on brightness and look nothing alike - which is exactly how a washed-out pig passed. The `Sat` and `Lum` checks exist because the rest of the table is blind by construction, not by oversight.
- **Redirected stdout is block-buffered, so an empty log does not mean a dead process.** Checking for a startup line 18 seconds into a run found a zero-byte file and looked like a crash; the process was healthy and simply had not filled its buffer. Check `Get-Process` before diagnosing, and read the log after the run has produced enough output — or close the game, which flushes.
- **A band narrower than the formula predicts is information about where the box is buried.** The wolf's body shows 6 columns where 12 are predicted, and crucially at `u+d+w` rather than `u+d` — so it is the *top* rect that is transparent, and after the torso's quarter turn that face points forward, which is where the mane must sit. Reading the missing rect placed a second box before any code was written. **Do not treat an unexpected width as a measurement error; work out which rect is absent and ask what covers it.**
- **Research the reference before designing a system, not after — and use the right model for the agents.** Two rounds of subagent research (2026-08-02/03) produced `RESEARCH.md`, and the first round was materially worse because the `model` parameter was omitted and the calls fell through to a small default; three of four reports had to be topped up by hand. **Always pass `model: "Claude Opus 5 (copilot)"`.** Launch several agents in one block so they run in parallel, and give each a tight scope with "exact numbers, note Java vs Bedrock, cite the page" in the prompt — the reports that asked for provenance came back with derivations that reproduce the wiki's published figures exactly, and the ones that did not came back as prose.
- **The villager's arm join is good enough and the user is done with it.** Direct signal, 2026-08-03: _"not perfect but eh, whatever i guess. i'm tired of iterating through hands."_ It is closed, not solved. **Do not re-open it unprompted** — and if it ever is re-opened, the remaining honest options are per-face culling for buried faces, or dropping the crossbar and accepting the loss of the crossed-arms pose. Nudging the numbers again is not one of them.
- **A drive that fades out as it approaches its target cannot oscillate, and a latch that does not fade cannot be gentle.** Tapering the kick to nothing as the head clears is first-order, so it settles dead - the player floats motionless, which was the *first* complaint. Latching it across a band cannot settle, but at the climb speed it overshoots enormously, which was the *second*. The answer is both: latch the band **and** drop the speed. Neither alone is a fix, and I shipped each of them alone once.
- **Vision and aiming are different questions and must not share a ray.** `seesPlayer` called `raycast`, which walks `selectionBoxes` - correct for "what am I pointing at", because you must be able to aim at a tuft of grass, and badly wrong for sight. **Tall grass therefore hid the player from a creeper**, so a Bramble crossing a meadow lost sight of you every few steps and its fuse stuttered up and down. `Block.hpp` has owned the right predicate the whole time: `isOpaque`, documented as *blocks vision* and deliberately kept distinct from *blocks movement*. `hasLineOfSight` now lives beside `raycast` so there is one ray walker and two questions, rather than two walkers.
- **A model that reads as "lying down" is usually missing a rotation, not a height.** The spider's body sat at 0.40 with its eight legs as level bars either side of it, and the comment justifying that said the reference "angles each leg down at the knee, which an axis-aligned box cannot do". It does not need a knee: the reference droops each whole leg 45 degrees (33.3 on the inner pairs) about its own forward axis from a joint level with the body, and **that droop is the entire reason the animal stands off the ground**. Rebuilt from `geometry.spider.v1.8`, where thorax, head, abdomen and all eight joints share one height. The tips end about a tenth of a block *under* the floor, which the reference does too and which is what makes it read as planted.
- **A positional table stops being a table once it outgrows a screen, and the tell is a field you cannot set without restating a dozen others.** `kSpecies` was thirty-six rows of bare values with "every field with a default goes at the end" as the rule holding it together. That rule works exactly until you need to set a late field on a row written before it existed — at which point you are retyping playtested numbers to reach the one you care about, which is the highest-risk edit in the codebase. Converting to designated initialisers cost nothing at runtime, made rows say only what makes them different, and turned a mis-ordered value from a silently wrong animal into a compile error. **Do this before the field you need forces you to, not after.**
- **The old distance hysteresis was standing in for something the reference does differently, and the honest fix was to copy the mechanism rather than keep the workaround.** `senseRange * 1.4` existed because a target sitting exactly on the sense boundary flipped every frame. Bedrock has no such widening: it scans on a `scan_interval` of ten ticks, so the question is simply not asked often enough to flicker. Taking the cadence removed the fudge *and* paid for a line-of-sight ray for the whole roster. **A constant with a comment explaining why it is a fudge is usually a missing mechanism.**
- **"Small and decorative" is not a reason to skip an axis, and the bug it caused looked nothing like the shortcut that caused it.** Dropped items resolved *vertically only*, documented as not worth the cost. So a drop could enter a block sideways - and once inside, the ground probe found **that** block and snapped the drop to its top, which the user reported as items climbing steps. **A partial resolver does not fail partially; it fails somewhere else entirely.** The fix was to call `overlapsSolid` and `highestSurfaceBelow` like everything else that collides, which is what `SYSTEM_MEMORY.md` had said to do all along.
